Doublet build: read ahead

Working brief for the two-doublet build. The reasoning is in _articles/two-doublets-2026-11.md; this is the short form to read before starting and to carry to the bench.

Station: 100 W SSB maximum, about 20 W digital. No amplifier planned.

1. The two antennas

  Portable inverted V Fixed flat top
Wire 132 ft, 22 AWG PolyStealth 88 ft, 14 AWG PolyStealth
Shape apex 40 ft, ends 10 ft horizontal, house to playhouse mast
Span needed 117 ft 88 ft
Apex angle 125 deg n/a
Feedline 300 ohm TV twin lead 300 ohm 18 AWG window line (DXE-LL300)
Balun one 2631828302, 9T identical
Lowest band 80 m, true half wave 40 m, 80 m through the tuner

Why these numbers

  • 132 ft is a half wave at 3.72 MHz in free space, nearer 3.55 MHz corrected. That is the lower half of 80 m, not the phone end, and it is not why the length was picked.
  • Its full-wave points do not all land above a band. Free space gives 7.45, 14.90, 22.35 and 29.82 MHz, all just clear. End effect moves them down: the one-wave point lands in 40 m (7.27 or 7.08) and the four-wave point in 10 m (29.64 or 29.45). 20 m and 15 m stay clear either way. Expect the high-impedance case on 40 m and 10 m, and sweep both in test D.
  • 88 ft beats 94 ft, but on the one-wave point, not the two-wave one. 94 ft’s two-wave point is 20.94 MHz, 64 kHz below 15 m and not inside it. Its one-wave point is 10.47 MHz free space and 10.21 or 9.95 corrected, astride a 30 m band 50 kHz wide. 88 ft clears both points under both corrections, by 355 kHz at the worst.
  • 14 AWG on the flat top buys 0.36 to 0.48 dB on 80 m over 22 AWG and almost nothing on other bands. Worth it on a fixed span, not worth the weight on a portable one.
  • 300 ohm on both. The 450 ohm advantage is 0.41 dB in one modeled corner (10 m into 3,000 ohm) and a tie on the other three. 300 ohm is lower voltage and caps the high excursion lower, and against the mAT-30’s 5 to 1,500 ohm window it wins on every load, by 1 to 9 points on the fraction of feedline lengths that land inside. The 450 ohm line’s extra excursion is all above the ceiling. The old “~95% either way” figure is withdrawn: it was an artifact of how the load range was sampled, and the ranking flipped between samplings. Wet weather costs ~1 dB, more than this choice does.

2. Shopping list

Already on hand: FT240-31 20 mm cores (2631828302), AWG 14 enamel, 22 AWG PolyStealth, 300 ohm TV twin lead, SO-239 connectors, NanoVNA, sleeve choke, mAT-30 tuner.

The tuner sets three numbers in this brief, from its manual: it tunes 5 to 1,500 ohm loads, takes 120 W SSB/CW peak and 30 W digital on HF, and covers 1.8 to 54 MHz. Its ANTENNA port is an SO-239, so the balun mounts straight to it. The manual’s power warning is unusually direct: power significantly over spec “will definitely damage or destroy” the tuner and may take the transceiver with it. 100 W SSB is 83% of the ceiling, 20 W digital 67%.

To buy:

  • 88 ft of 14 AWG PolyStealth, plus spare
  • 300 ohm 18 AWG window line, DXE-LL300, start with 65 ft (section 5). Nominal is 272 ohm, VF 0.88. Measure it, card A.
  • 2 plastic project boxes, roughly 4 x 4 x 2 in
  • Screw terminals or eye bolts, 2 pairs
  • #14 PTFE-insulated hookup wire, ~5 ft, for the two balun windings. Record maker, part number, OD and rated voltage. The 88 ohm figure assumes a 2.40 mm OD and the voltage argument assumes a real wall; both vary by maker across the five options in section 8
  • Center insulators, 2. A ladder-line mount such as the WA1FFL Ladder-Loc carries the line and the wires on separate paths
  • End insulators, shock cord or a pulley and weight
  • Plastic standoffs for line support. An inch of clearance is a rule of thumb, not a specification; take the DXE-LL300 guidance for the actual line and give it as much room as the run allows
  • Short length of good coax for the jumper, inches not feet. The mAT-30 manual calls for 50 ohm coax “rated 120 watts or greater” on its radio side; the output-side jumper carries the transformed impedance, so rate it against 775 V peak. Check the SO-239 too: the format is not a rating

3. Build order

  1. Balun one. Wind it, run bench tests A, B, C before building the second.
  2. Flat top, since it is the fixed installation. Wire, center insulator, line, then test D and E.
  3. Balun two, once the first design is confirmed.
  4. Portable V last.

4. Balun build card

One Fair-Rite 2631828302. Nine close-wound turns of #14 PTFE hookup wire. Two cores build both baluns, one each.

   
Wound line 0.65 m per conductor
Predicted choke R 2.0 to 3.9 kohm, 80 m to 10 m
Target Zo of the wound pair 88 ohm, close wound, 2.40 mm center spacing

Close-wound #14 PTFE hookup wire. No sleeve, no spacer. The insulation wall sets the spacing by itself: about 2.40 mm between centers, lands near 88 ohms. W2WDX builds several dozen this way and K6JCA measures ordinary #14 bifilar at 60 to 100 ohms. One part, no lacing step.

Wire OD Zo 9T fill
bare enamel (on hand) 1.75 mm 43 ohm 30%
#14 PTFE standard wall 2.40 mm 88 ohm 41%
#14 PTFE thin wall 2.13 mm 74 ohm 37%
#14 silicone 3.20 mm 107 ohm 57%
#14 THHN 2.90 mm 88 ohm 51%

Bare enamel also works and is what LDG and M0UKD build. The reason to buy PTFE is the 894 V peak this station can put at the balun against a real insulation wall rather than a film, plus abrasion at the bore edge. Not performance.

The withdrawn-cord air-gap version is dropped. It reached ~203 ohms and nothing in the field is built that way: LDG and M0UKD close-wind bare enamel, W2WDX and DX Engineering use thick PTFE insulation, Balun Designs sleeves. Every one is a bifilar pair of insulated wire, 9 to 18 turns, on one or two FT240-class cores, spaced only by the insulation. The cord variant also cost bore space and loosened the coupling the balun works by.

Test C still runs, but it decides an optimization and not whether the thing works. A tuner inches away matches any of these readings.

Winding. W1JR crossover: half the turns from one side, cross over the outside, the rest from the other, so the two ends finish physically apart.

Connections. One transmission line, four ends, nothing else. No third winding, no link between the two wires, no ground on the balanced side.

End Goes to
Wire A in coax center
Wire B in coax shield
Wire A out balanced terminal 1
Wire B out balanced terminal 2

Hardware. Screw terminals through the box wall so the line’s tension is on hardware, not a solder joint. SO-239 on the coax end. Line enters away from the coax, neither run near the other or near metal.

Bore fill, close-wound #14 PTFE (4.80 mm ribbon, 106 mm of bore):

Turns Fill Gap between pairs  
9 41% 6.8 mm the build
12 55% 3.9 mm fine
14 65% 2.6 mm fine

The dropped air-spaced version was 84% at 14 turns. Close winding removes the turn-count constraint entirely.

Escalation if the measurement says it is not enough: 12 turns on the same single core gives 3.6 to 6.9 kohm for 0.86 m of line, and that is as far as one core goes. Past twelve, a second core is the only route: stacking makes the bore longer, not narrower, so nine turns on two stacked is the same 54% fill as nine on one.

5. Where the balun goes, which matters more than what it is

N6BV, 40 m inverted V at 14.1 MHz, 1,500 W, power reaching the antenna:

Arrangement To the antenna
Balanced tuner, open wire all the way 1,325 W
Choke at the unbalanced tuner’s 50 ohm input 1,314 W
Balun at the window, 20 ft coax jumper 397 W

The third row is the common build and it loses 696 W in the jumper. Scaled to 100 W that is 46 W, and the fraction does not change with power.

So: balun bolted to the tuner’s antenna connector. Jumper in inches. A second choke, the sleeve choke, goes between the radio and the tuner.

The coax-only tuner is what forces this. A balanced or link-coupled tuner is the upgrade the measurement argues for.

6. Measurement cards

A. Feedline Zo and velocity factor

Both lines, before anything else, because “300 ohm” line is nominally 272 and “450 ohm” line is usually nearer 400.

Take 20 to 30 ft, sweep 1 to 30 MHz twice, far end shorted then open.

  • vf = 2 * L * f / c from the first impedance minimum with the end shorted
  • Zo = sqrt(Zoc * Zsc) at one frequency, repeated at several to check

Keep the far-end short physically short. Inches of wire is an inductor at 28 MHz.

B. Balun common-mode impedance

Series-through, port 1 to both conductors shorted at one end, port 2 to both shorted at the other, normalized against the shorted-clips reference.

Zcm = 2 * Z0 * (1 - S21) / S21     S21 complex and linear, not dB

Grade on resistance, not magnitude. Expect 2.0 to 3.9 kohm.

C. Transparency test, the one that decides the design

300 ohm non-inductive resistor across the balanced terminals, S11 from the coax end, 1 to 30 MHz.

Band Should read
80 m 287 − 58j
40 m 258 − 99j
20 m 184 − 136j
15 m 126 − 132j
10 m 88 − 115j

Near 88 − 115j at 10 m is consistent with the assumed wall and velocity factor. Near 34 − 86j is consistent with a thinner wall behaving like bare enamel at ~43 ohms. Neither reading proves a wall thickness on its own: winding geometry, parasitic capacitance, core coupling and the fixture all move it. What the test establishes is the transformation the finished winding actually applies, which is the thing the old coax-wound balun got wrong. Either reading still works into the tuner; the low one is an optimization missed, not a failed build. Record the whole sweep, not one number per band.

Optional test C2, balance: two matched 150 ohm non-inductive resistors in series across the terminals, junction to the coax shield. Should read 300 ohms with the junction grounded and floating; the difference is the balance error. Below 30 MHz the fixture is most of this measurement, so pin it down before trusting a number: carbon composition or thin-film resistors matched on a meter first, leads under half an inch, the ground connection a direct strap to the shell rather than a clip lead, and the calibration plane at the balun’s own connector. A result that moves when the leads move is the fixture talking.

D. What the tuner will see

  1. Calibrate open, short, load at the connector that plugs into the tuner, jumper in place.
  2. Clip the sleeve choke onto the jumper between NanoVNA and balun.
  3. Connect jumper, balun, feedline, antenna. Sweep 3 to 30 MHz.
  4. Record R and X at each band center, and at both edges of 80 m.
  5. Flag any band where R falls outside 5 to 1,500 ohms, the mAT-30’s rated load range.

That is the real window, from the mAT-30 manual: “Tunes 5 to 1500 ohm loads.” It is narrower at the top than the 20 to 2,000 placeholder this brief used to carry, and wider at the bottom. It is also only half a criterion: the manual gives a resistive-load range and says nothing about how much reactance comes with it, and 100 ohm with a few ohms reactive is not the same job as 100 ohm with 2,000 ohms reactive. Record X at every band center alongside R, and treat a band near 1,500 ohm with a large X as a flag even though R is inside.

If touching the jumper moves the trace, the reading is common-mode current, not the antenna. Add choking until it stops moving.

E. Choosing the feedline length

Add line in a few-foot increments, sweep again, keep the table with the fewest bands outside range. A switchable insertion loop makes this a minute per trial. First length that puts every band inside range wins; there is no optimum to chase across eight bands.

Starting lengths, from the ARRL feedline-plus-half-dipole table:

Target sum 132 ft V 88 ft flat top
75 ± 2 ft too short 31 ft
95.5 ± 3 ft 29.5 ft 51.5 ft
109 ± 3 ft 43 ft 65 ft
145 ± 3 ft 79 ft 101 ft

Avoid a line a half wavelength long or any multiple: it reproduces the feedpoint impedance at the shack end, and on the high-impedance bands that is several thousand ohms.

What D and E cannot show: the impedance at the tuner says nothing about how much power got there. Feel the balun after operating, not during. A warm balun is converting transmitted energy into heat.

8. Settled, do not relitigate

  • 1:1, not 4:1. A 4:1 helps where impedance is reliably high and hurts where it is low, and a doublet is low on some bands by construction.
  • A ratio is not chosen from the line’s Zo. A 4:1 fed from 450 ohm line is not matching 450 to 50. The “450 ohm needs a 9:1” idea is a myth.
  • Mix 31, not the FT240-43s. 31 peaks low and holds through the bottom of HF, which is where an 80 m choke works.
  • One core. Settled on power, by a factor of ten. PA9X rates a bifilar FT240 in AWG 18 PTFE at 1 kW SSB and ~200 W continuous digital; this is 100 W SSB and 20 W digital on heavier AWG 14, on a 20 mm core worth 1.57 standard FT240s. KV5R runs a Balun Designs 1171 cool at 1 kW on a ladder-line doublet. The field stacks (M0UKD, W2WDX) to buy power handling this station does not need. Stacking also gives ~10% more wound line for the same choking impedance. The margin is an inference across builds, since PA9X’s figure is a different wire gauge on its own geometry and a choke’s power handling tracks the common-mode voltage it sees as much as the ferrite; a factor of ten carries that, and the warm-balun check after test D confirms it. Revised 2026-11-25.
  • SO-239 throughout.
  • No amplifier, so no 1,500 W margins anywhere.

9. Still unknown

  • The choke impedance is scaled from one measurement of nine turns of RG400 on one of these cores. The scaling is standard; the winding is different.
  • How much reactance the mAT-30 tolerates at a given resistance. “5 to 1500 ohm loads” is a resistive figure and the manual gives no R-X chart, so the only way to find the edge is test D on the real antenna.
  • Which side of the band edge the 132 ft wire’s one-wave and four-wave points land on. Both corrections put them in 40 m and 10 m and disagree by 190 kHz about where, and neither accounts for height, ground or the bend. A NEC model of the real geometry would settle it before the wire goes up.
  • What the finished winding’s Zo actually is. 88 ohm is computed from a nominal 2.40 mm spacing and an assumed effective permittivity, and spacing varies around a toroid. Close winding removed the question of holding a gap open; it did not answer this one. Test C decides.
  • Whether the PolyStealth cladding is three skin depths thick at 3.75 MHz (102 micrometers). If it is thinner, the 80 m wire loss is worse than the table.
  • What either antenna does at this height over this ground. The published impedance tables are for a 131 ft doublet at 33 ft and an 88 ft dipole at 30 ft, neither of which is this installation.
  • Whether the ARRL length table means anything here. Wide tolerances, a 1999 reprint of an older chart.

The failure that would change the plan: test D showing a band that no feedline length brings inside the tuner’s range. That means a different wire length, or accepting the antenna does not cover that band.
