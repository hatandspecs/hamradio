A Beacon That Has to Own the Radio: WSPR on the Cyberdeck

Published on: October 17, 2026  · 

The ham radio cyberdeck exists to do one thing: the ham radio that otherwise needs a desktop, a mouse, and a graphical program that makes the hobby feel like a second shift. It started with keyboard-to-keyboard digimodes because that was the itch at the time. A logbook followed, and the two became peers on a ring you move around with ^O and ^P.

WSPR is the opposite kind of operating. Nobody is at the keyboard. The radio transmits a 110 second beacon into a two minute slot, or it listens, and the interesting part happens hours later on somebody else’s receiver. There is no conversation to have.

It is also the digital mode I have the most hours on, and the one I reach for first. Every antenna I have built has been judged by it — the 75-10 end-fed, the 40 m center-loaded dipole, the field day dipoles. You put a wire up, you beacon for an afternoon, and the map tells you what you actually built rather than what the modeling software hoped you built. For antenna work there is nothing else that gives you that many independent receivers for free.

So the awkward thing to admit is that until recently I did all of it from a laptop, and I did not enjoy any of it.

That is not WSPR’s fault. It is that WSJT-X and fldigi both assume a desk, a mouse, a waterfall, and a stack of windows — and sitting down to that, in the evening, after a day of sitting down to exactly that, feels like clocking back in. I built the cyberdeck to get the conversation modes away from that feeling, and it worked better than I expected: I only discovered I liked digital modes once the operating position stopped looking like an office.

Which left WSPR — the mode I use most — as the last thing still tethered to the laptop.

What I did not expect was that bringing it over would be governed, start to finish, by one fact about a sound card.

The deck beaconing on 20 m, with the FTX-1 above it and a common-mode choke in the foreground. 455 stations heard in that session.

One sound card, one client

The deck drives fldigi, and fldigi holds the USB codec. WSPR needs that same codec — to record 114 seconds of audio for wsprd, and to play a rendered beacon back out.

I assumed, reasonably, that two programs could share it. They cannot. With fldigi running:

$ arecord -l
  ... Subdevices: 0/1

$ arecord -D plughw:1,0 -f S16_LE -r 12000 -c 1 -d 5 /tmp/x.wav
arecord: main:850: audio open error: Device or resource busy

There is no dmix or dsnoop to share through — no /etc/asound.conf, no ~/.asoundrc. One capture client, and fldigi is it.

So WSPR cannot be a screen you visit while the digimodes keep running in the background. It has to stop fldigi, take the card, and give both back when it is done. That single constraint produced everything else: WSPR is a fourth ring area and the only exclusive one. While it runs the ring refuses to leave it, and the message says so rather than the key doing nothing.

That is a worse user experience than the alternative, and I could not find an honest way around it.

Making a signal the real decoder would accept

WSPR packs a callsign, a four character grid and a power level into 50 bits, convolutionally encodes them to 162 symbols, interleaves, and adds a sync vector — one bit per symbol, 162 of them, fixed by the protocol.

I needed that vector. Rather than copy it out of a document I could misread, I pulled it out of the decoder binary I was already going to ship:

$ strings -t d /usr/bin/wsprd | ...

It appears twice in wsprd, and both copies agree: 162 values, 63 of them ones. Two independent copies in the artifact that will judge my output is better evidence than one table in a PDF.

Then the real test. Generate a window with my encoder, write it to a WAV with the filename wsprd expects, and hand it to the actual decoder:

The deck’s own generated window, decoded by the real wsprd. Four decodes, 20 MiB peak, 14.5 seconds normal and 33.7 deep.

18 dB KD3CCO FN10 37. The encoder was right, and I knew it because the program that would be reading my signal on the air read it on the bench.

One detail worth keeping: wsprd takes the timestamp from the filename, not from the file. I proved it by renaming 261017_0208.wav to 991231_2358.wav and watching the reported time follow. YYMMDD_HHMM.wav is load-bearing.

Getting the decoder onto a 512 MB board

wsprd is not packaged on its own — it ships inside WSJT-X. Installing the whole package on a Pi 3A+ costs 36 MB and drags in a Qt GUI that will never be displayed.

It is a 55 KB binary. Extracting just that, plus libgfortran5 because it is Fortran:

apt-get download wsjtx
dpkg-deb -x wsjtx_*.deb /tmp/wsjtx
sudo install -m 755 /tmp/wsjtx/usr/bin/wsprd /usr/local/bin/wsprd
sudo apt-get install -y libgfortran5

About 1 MB all in. On the deck it decodes a 120 second slot in 14.5 seconds normally, 33.7 with deep search, at 20 MiB peak.

Two measurements I got wrong

Both of these would have changed a decision, and both looked convincing at the time.

The memory figure. My first benchmark reported wsprd peaking at 315 MiB. On a 512 MB board that is close to fatal, and I nearly designed around it — decode on alternate slots, or not at all. The number came from resource.getrusage(RUSAGE_CHILDREN).ru_maxrss, which counts memory the child inherited at fork() before exec replaced it. I was measuring my own benchmark harness. /usr/bin/time -v says 22 MiB. The feature was never in danger; my measurement was.

The audio level. An early capture read −59.7 dBFS, far below what wsprd wants, and I wrote down that the capture gain was about 35 dB low. The radio was connected to a dummy load. There was no signal to measure — a terminated antenna port produces exactly that reading. With an antenna on a live 40 m it reads −36.8 dBFS and decodes fine.

The pattern in both is the same: a number produced by a real tool, in the right units, that answers a different question than the one I asked.

The screen

The WSPR screen, drawn by the same code the panel runs. Eight spots on 20 m, the state block below them, and the slot clock five seconds into a two minute window.

The distance and bearing columns are computed from the two grid squares — WSPR carries a grid and no distance, so wsprnet and wspr.rocks fill their own kilometer column the same way. I validated mine against my own wsprnet records rather than against the formula alone: agreement inside 0.5 km on every six character path from 498 to 6855 km. Neither figure is stored in the log, because both are derivable from the grids that are.

The power line is the one I am most pleased with. WSPR cannot encode an arbitrary wattage — the field holds a dBm from a fixed set, so only 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 W land exactly. The deck reads what the radio is actually making over CAT, shows the dBm it will encode to, and highlights it when the two agree. Adjust the power control and watch for the highlight. 3 W is 34.77 dBm and goes out as 33, which is a small lie told on your behalf unless you can see it happening.

Setting the receive level is its own screen, because a level set without a meter to watch is a number changed rather than a level set:

Calibrating the receive gain. The marked stretch of the meter is roughly what wsprd wants; + and - move the capture mixer while it updates.

That gain is the ALSA capture mixer, which fldigi also uses — which is safe only because WSPR owns the codec exclusively while it runs. The deck reads the old value before it writes, and puts it back when the session ends.

Three small truths that cost a day

The sound card index moves. The codec was card 1 one morning and card 2 after a reboot, with HDMI taking 1. Everything addressed as plughw:1,0 was then pointed at the HDMI output, and arecord failed with No such file or directory — a different error from the busy one, and one that no amount of stopping fldigi fixes. Worse, the capture gain control had been aimed at the HDMI card too, so it had been silently doing nothing while the screen reported a percentage it had never managed to set. Everything now addresses the card by name: plughw:CARD=Device,DEV=0.

sudo does not work from inside a systemd service. Stopping fldigi needs authorization. A NOPASSWD sudoers rule parses, verifies from a login shell, and still fails from inside cyberdeck-ui — which has no tty, no PAM session, and a capability bounding set narrowed to CAP_SYS_TTY_CONFIG. The answer was polkit, which is what the deck already uses to let the WiFi screen talk to NetworkManager, for exactly the same reason. The comment at the top of that older rule describes the failure precisely; I had read it months ago and still spent two deploys on sudo.

Ctrl-I is Tab. I put the transmit interlock on Ctrl-I for “inhibit”. They are the same byte — ASCII 9 — and no terminal can tell them apart, least of all the Linux console. So Tab was arming and inhibiting the transmitter, including on a form where Tab means “next field”. It is Ctrl-B now, and byte 9 toggles nothing.

What it heard, and who heard it

819 spots in one afternoon. 710 on 20 m, 108 on 40 m.

Five watts on 40 m reached 43 receivers in a single slot. On 20 m it reached ON5KQ in Belgium at 6060 km, HK4CZE in Colombia, and the entire US west coast.

Receiving is the half I find more satisfying, because it is the half that needed the decoder to work: eleven stations in one slot, including K5NAV at 0.2 W over 2781 km. That is 13,905 km per watt, decoded on a Raspberry Pi 3A+ by a 55 KB binary extracted out of a package I did not install.

The deck’s own spots on wspr.rocks, reporting version = cyberdeck.

The uploader reports itself as cyberdeck rather than borrowing a WSJT-X version string. Claiming to be software you are not makes every bug reported against you somebody else’s problem. The field holds ten characters, which I know because a reporter calling itself EasySpotter appears in my records as EasySpotte.

What is not done

The time-out timer disarms transmit and leaves the receiver running — the timer is about an unattended transmitter, and listening costs nothing. That was a late change and it has not yet run its full hour unattended.

Band hopping works and the rotation is set from the panel, but I have not left it hopping overnight, which is the case it exists for.

And a type 2 or type 3 message — the compound and hashed callsigns, W3USR/05 and friends — still rests on hand written test fixtures. My decoder parses them. I have not yet transmitted one.
