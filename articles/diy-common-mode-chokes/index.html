Common-Mode Chokes, Not Grounds: Why a Shield Has To Be Interrupted

Published on: October 31, 2026  · 

A coax feedline has three conductors, not two. There is the center conductor, there is the inside surface of the shield, and there is the outside surface of the shield, and the third one is not part of the transmission line.

At HF, skin depth in copper is a few thousandths of an inch, far thinner than the braid, so current on the inside surface and current on the outside surface are electrically independent. The inside surface carries the return current of the transmission line, and that pair carries power to the antenna. The outside surface carries whatever else it happens to carry, and whatever it carries it radiates and receives, because it is a wire running from the antenna into the house.

Common-mode current is the current on that third conductor. A choke is the only practical way to control it. The two things usually reached for instead, a ground rod and a bonding strap, do not address it.

Building and measuring one is a separate article: making a transmit-grade common-mode choke.

Where the current comes from

Asymmetry at the antenna. A dipole is a balanced load and coax is an unbalanced line. Connect one to the other directly and the return current arrives at the feedpoint and divides between the far half of the dipole and the outside of the shield, because those are two conductors in parallel and the current has no reason to prefer one. How it divides depends on height, ground, nearby objects and feedline length. None of that is controlled by bonding the other end.

The same thing happens at an end-fed half-wave. The antenna wire is one conductor, the unun’s cold terminal needs a return path, and if no return has been provided deliberately then the return is the shield’s outer surface. The feedline becomes the counterpoise by default.

Field pickup along the run. Even with a perfectly symmetric feedpoint, the outer surface is a conductor sitting in the antenna’s near field, and in everyone else’s. It picks up from the antenna, from co-located transmitters, and, the one that matters most on receive, from every noisy device in the building whose field it passes through on the way to the operating position.

Both mechanisms produce the same symptoms: RF in the shack, hot microphone casings, control gear that resets on transmit, an SWR reading that changes when the feedline moves, a pattern that does not match the model, and a noise floor that includes the building.

Why grounding does not fix it

“Ground it” is the standard advice for almost every other electrical problem. Two things make it the wrong advice here.

At RF there is no ground nearby. Ten feet of #6 wire to a rod is about 4.4 microhenries, and its impedance is almost entirely inductive reactance:

Band Reactance of a 10 ft #6 ground wire
160 m about 50 ohms
80 m about 100 ohms
40 m about 200 ohms
20 m about 390 ohms
10 m about 790 ohms

Flat strap instead of round wire reduces that by tens of percent, not by orders of magnitude. Ten feet is also a quarter wavelength at about 24 MHz, and a quarter-wave conductor grounded at the far end presents a high impedance at the near end, so on 12 m that ground wire is closer to an open circuit than to a ground, and it is a quarter-wave radiator fed at its base by the common-mode current it was installed to drain.

The common-mode current is not trying to get to ground. It is flowing on a conductor that has become part of an antenna. A ground connection is a shunt path, and a shunt only diverts current if it offers a much lower impedance than the alternative. A choke is a series impedance inserted in the unwanted path itself, and a series impedance reduces the current in that path no matter where the current was trying to go. A current whose path has not been interrupted cannot be shunted away, and a ground rod does not interrupt the shield.

None of this argues against bonding, which is mandatory and does a different, essential job:

Job Done by Not done by
Carrying fault current so a breaker trips AC safety ground A choke
Diverting lightning energy to earth Arrestors bonded to a single-point entry panel and a rod A choke
Holding enclosures at the same potential Bonding A choke
Keeping RF current off the outside of a coax shield A choke A ground rod, a bonding strap, or a counterpoise alone

A station with correct grounding still has its full common-mode problem.

High-RF sites: noise floor, receiver overload, and feedline coupling

Everything above applies in a quiet backyard. A site with high field strength, meaning a hilltop, a shared tower, a dense suburb near broadcast or paging transmitters, or a second radio on the same roof, adds three consequences on top of it.

On receive, the shield’s outer surface is an antenna galvanically connected to the receiver’s input. In a building full of switching supplies that conductor passes through every one of their fields on its way to the rig. This is why the receive test in a site survey is to insert a choke and read the noise floor again: if the number drops, the shield was the path, and no amount of work on the antenna would have found it.

At a strong-signal site, common-mode pickup adds to the total power at the front end. A direct-sampling receiver has a finite input range, and out-of-band carriers arriving along the shield count against it as the wanted signal does. Choking can reduce overload and intermod even when it does nothing audible to the noise floor.

On transmit, common-mode current couples one feedline to another. Two runs on the same tower or through the same entry panel form a coupled pair. That is a desense path between two radios that no filtering at either rig will fix, because the coupling happens outside both of them.

The current that produces a buzz at 5 W produces burns on a microphone at 100 W and shuts down a rotator controller at 500 W.

Where chokes go

A choke belongs wherever the common-mode path crosses a boundary that matters: the feedpoint, the building entry, and the rig. More than one is normal, because a choke only controls the current that passes through it, so one at the shack does nothing about a feedline radiating in the backyard.

Location Why there Watch out for
Shack side of the lightning arrestor at the entry panel The panel is where the shield is deliberately bonded, and everything past it runs through the house Put the entry-panel choke on the shack side. A choke ahead of the arrestor is an inductor in series with the surge path, so it raises the impedance of the route to the diverter and takes the voltage itself
At the rig, tuner or amplifier input Catches current picked up inside the shack, on jumpers and in the cable bundle The easiest to add and the least effective on its own
Dipole, doublet or loop feedpoint The asymmetry originates at the feedpoint, and without choking an impedance measurement means little Weight and weatherproofing
Vertical feedpoint, above the radial plate Keeps the shield from becoming an unintended extra radial Does not substitute for radials
End-fed half-wave, at the unun with a counterpoise on its ground terminal The counterpoise carries the return current, so the feedline no longer has to Provide the return before isolating the feedline
Rig DC cord, rotator cable, control and network cables They are conductors in the same field and they carry current too Snap-ons are fine; measure before and after

That preference does not extend to the feedpoint chokes below. Every choke at an antenna is on the antenna side of the entry panel, because the entry panel is at the house. A dipole, vertical or EFHW choke is unavoidably in the surge path and there is no alternative position for it. What follows is that those chokes are surge-exposed hardware: wind them on a core that will not shatter, keep the turns spread so a flashover has somewhere not to go, and expect to inspect them after a close strike. The rule is about not adding a second, avoidable inductance immediately in front of the arrestor, not about the surge path being choke-free.

The end-fed half-wave case needs stating plainly. A choke right at the unun and nothing else is the arrangement that looks most careful, and it is the one that fails. An EFHW is fed at a high-impedance point through a 49:1 or 56:1 unun. The feedpoint current is small but not zero and it needs somewhere to go. Two arrangements handle it deliberately: a choke at the unun plus a dedicated counterpoise on the unun’s ground terminal, or a choke placed a deliberate distance down the feedline with that length of shield serving as the counterpoise. Either way the counterpoise runs around 0.05 wavelength at the lowest band, roughly 14 feet for 80 m and 7 feet for 40 m, and it is worth trimming and recording. My own 75-10 m EFHW uses the first, with a 12-turn FT240-43 choke at the unun and a counterpoise that settled at 8.75 feet.

A choke at the unun with no counterpoise starves the antenna of a return path. The choke’s impedance is then in series with the return current the feedpoint requires, so the match degrades, the tuning moves, and power that should have been radiated is dissipated in the choke. Provide the return, then isolate the feedline.

How the feedline changes how much choking is needed

How much common-mode current exists is a property of the whole conductive assembly, not of the antenna alone.

Very short coax runs are not automatically safe. The common-mode conductor is the coax plus everything bonded to it. A three-foot jumper from an EFHW to a handheld is one of the worst cases there is, because the counterpoise it finds is the radio, the operator’s hand, and the operator. The coax is electrically short; the assembly is not. Portable and QRP setups frequently need a choke and a defined counterpoise more urgently than a home station with 100 feet of buried coax.

Long runs raise the need for three reasons. A 100-foot run is several quarter-wavelengths somewhere in HF, so on some band the common-mode path is near resonance, which is the condition that produces the largest current. It has far more surface exposed to building noise. And it usually passes near other cables. Long runs also want more than one choke, because common-mode current forms a standing wave with maxima roughly every half wavelength and a choke at a current null does little.

Twin lead and open-wire line change the geometry but not the problem. There is no third surface, but common-mode current still exists as a net in-phase current on the two conductors together, created by asymmetry: unequal coupling to nearby metal, an unbalanced source, or a line routed along a wall. Three consequences follow. The critical interface moves to the station end, where an unbalanced rig meets a balanced line, and that junction needs a genuine current balun or a link-coupled balanced tuner rather than a voltage balun. Routing matters more than it does with coax. And high SWR means high common-mode voltage at the station end, which is hard on ferrite: core heating and, in bad cases, flashover between turns. That last is the one case where a balanced tuner beats a bigger choke.

Feedline Effect What to do
Short coax, fixed station, return provided Usually less current One choke at the feedpoint; measure before adding more
Short coax, portable or handheld Often severe; the radio and operator become the counterpoise Choke plus a deliberate counterpoise, at the antenna
Medium run, under a wavelength Depends on antenna symmetry Feedpoint and entry
Long run, several wavelengths Higher; resonance somewhere in HF is near-certain Both ends, and sweep for a current maximum between
Twin lead or open-wire No shield current, but asymmetry still radiates Current balun or balanced tuner at the station end
Twin lead at high SWR High common-mode voltage stresses ferrite Balanced tuner or link coupling in preference to a large choke

What a choke has to be

A choke is a high impedance in series with the shield’s outer surface, and it has to be large, resistive, and present on every band the station uses.

Resistive rather than reactive, because an inductive choke stores energy and gives it back. In series with a common-mode path that has its own reactance, a purely inductive choke can series-resonate with it, and at that frequency the total impedance falls and the current goes up. A resistive choke dissipates the energy instead and cannot resonate with the line. This is why ferrite mixes are chosen for their loss rather than their permeability, and why a measurement reporting only a magnitude cannot grade one.

Mix 31 is the usual choice for HF: its complex permeability is strongly resistive across the low and middle HF bands. Mix 43 is less lossy at the bottom of HF and better at the top and into VHF. Mix 61 is a VHF material with little useful loss at HF.

Dissipation is worst when the choke’s impedance is comparable to the path impedance, so a mediocre choke runs hotter than either a bad one or a good one. The fixes are to make the choke much larger than the path, or to reduce the current at its source by fixing the asymmetry. A choke that gets hot is reporting common-mode current that was already there.

Style Good for Watch out
Coax on a ferrite toroid The default HF transmit choke Turn count trades low-band impedance against self-resonance
Two such chokes in series More resistance, and more impedance at the top of HF than one winding gives Gains nothing at the top of HF unless the two ends are kept well apart
Stacked toroids under one winding More impedance and thermal mass at the same turn count Heavy, and it helps only where resistance is the limit
Snap-on clamps or a bead string Receive chokes, DC cords, control cables, quick tests Impedance adds per bead, so one clamp is rarely enough
Bifilar-wound 1:1 current balun Balanced feedpoints and tuner outputs Must be transmission-line wound, not an autotransformer
Coax coil with no ferrite Very little Narrow and highly reactive; its impedance collapses off resonance

Jim Brown K9YC’s published notes on ferrites and common-mode chokes are the standard reference for specific recipes.

Confirming a choke works in the station

A choke’s bench impedance is necessary and not sufficient. The bench measures the component, not the circuit. A 5 kohm choke in a path whose common-mode impedance happens to be 2 kohm buys about 11 dB, not 34, and that path impedance cannot be measured with any instrument likely to be in a home station. Placement matters as much: a perfect choke at a current null does nothing.

So confirm the install with two measurements:

  • On transmit, with a clamp-on RF current probe: sweep along the line, record the peak, install the choke, sweep again. A good install cuts the peak by 20 dB or more.
  • On receive, with the noise-floor test in Phase 3 of the site survey: read the floor, insert the choke at the entry, read it again. A drop of 3 dB or more means the shield was conducting building noise into the receiver.

Both are differences, and both should be compared against the measurement repeatability before being believed. A 2 dB improvement measured with a 3 dB spread between repeat readings has not been shown to be an improvement.
