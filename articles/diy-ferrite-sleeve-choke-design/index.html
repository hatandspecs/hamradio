Eleven Ferrite Chokes Wound and Measured, and Why I Could Not Predict Any of Them

Published on: November 14, 2026  · 

Where this stands. Eleven four-core common-mode chokes wound on Fair-Rite sleeves with RG-316, each swept 1 to 30 MHz, plus eighteen single-sleeve sweeps behind them and one re-wind of the best build with tidier leads. Eleven built assemblies is enough to test a design model instead of trusting one, and that test is most of what this article records.

Seven of the eleven hold 5 kilohms of resistance on every band from 80 m through 10 m. The best holds 6,858 ohms and stays resistance-dominant throughout. It is four separate single-core sections: two mix-75 sleeves at eight turns for the low end, a four-turn mix 31 and a five-turn mix 43 for the high end.

Re-winding that one build with tidier leads added 52 percent at 10 m and nothing at all below 20 m, which is the clearest single result in this work and the only thing here that improved a choke rather than ruling something out.

No model in this work predicted any of it. Five independent prediction routes were tried against the measured builds and all five failed by 23 to 47 percent on the one number a build is chosen by. Three chokes picked by the best of those models came in 24, 31 and 75 percent below prediction. The second half of this article is the record of that failure, because it turned out to be more useful than the predictions were.

This article is RG-316 only. RG-400 does not work in any four-core design on these cores, because not enough turns fit through the bore to reach the resistance wanted, so everything below is RG-316.

The criterion. At least 5 kilohms of resistance on every band, and resistance rather than reactance. Common-mode current falls as the choke’s impedance rises against the antenna system’s own common-mode impedance, which for a wire antenna and feedline sits in the hundreds of ohms to low thousands; a choke an order of magnitude above that stops being the limit. Resistance matters separately because a choke that is mostly reactance stores energy and hands it back, and moves the resonance of whatever it is attached to. A choke is resistance-dominant where R is at least the magnitude of X, which is R/|Z| at or above 0.707. Every figure here is the real part.

How they were measured

The series-through fixture. The choke under test clips between the two posts, and the bare wire alongside is the reference.

A NanoVNA-F V2, SOLT calibrated, series-through fixture, 1 to 30 MHz at 301 points, with Zcm = 2·Z0·(1 − S21)/S21.

The fixture residual is subtracted here, and divided out in the toroid choke article, and both are right. How to remove a reference depends on what the reference is. For a series element S21 = 2·Z0/(2·Z0 + Z), and two series elements compose as Z1 + Z2, which is not multiplicative in S21, so division returns Z/(1 + Za/2·Z0) rather than Z − Za. Division is exact instead for a delay, which contributes phase at unit magnitude.

The discriminator is |S21|: a delay holds it at 1, a lumped reactance drops it as frequency rises. Fitting both models to each reference over 8 to 29 MHz settles it. The compact short used for the single toroid choke, fixture ends brought together, is a 0.85 ns delay and fits that model 2.3 times better, so that article divides. The long wire spanning this build’s geometry is a 453 nH lumped inductance and fits that model 1.9 times better, so this one subtracts. An earlier version of this article divided a lumped reference, which was wrong for this fixture. choke_s2p.py now runs the fit and picks on its own, printing which it chose.

Which reference matters, and one reference covers all eleven. The assembly geometry’s short measures 455 nH of residual inductance against 289 nH at the single-sleeve geometry, so sweep SJ000046 and not SJ000026 is the correct subtraction for every choke above. All eleven were swept the same day in the same fixture at the same lead distances, and the re-wind the following day against that same reference, with the fixture and calibration untouched.

The fixture’s ceiling runs 2.1 MΩ at 160 m to 173 kΩ at 10 m. Every reading below is 0 to 9 percent of that ceiling, so nothing here is the fixture measuring itself.

Watch out. No measured number in this article comes from above a winding’s own resonance. The two de-embedding conventions differ by a factor of 40 there: for mix 31 at eight turns at 28.4 MHz, subtraction gives 2,283 ohms and division gives 54 for the same sweep, and the material model says

The two halves of the criterion disagree, and that disagreement is the result. Choke 11 has the highest worst-band resistance of anything wound, 7,369 ohms, but its R/|Z| falls to 0.59 at 10 m, so most of what it presents there is reactance it will hand back. Seven chokes clear 5 kilohms on every band. Only two stay resistance-dominant while doing it.

The best measuring configurations

Choke 2 is the best choke wound. #75@8T + #75@8T + #31@4T + #43@5T, 6,706 ohms on its worst band as first built, R/|Z| never below 0.76, 5 kilohms cleared everywhere from 80 m to 10 m. Re-wound with tidier leads it reaches 6,858 ohms with R/|Z| never below 0.89, which is the build to copy.

Choke 1 is a near-equal second and is arguably better balanced. #75@8T + #75@8T + #43@5T + #43@5T, 6,307 ohms worst. Its weak band is 80 m rather than 10 m, which makes it the flatter of the two across the high bands, and it reaches 12,286 ohms at 17 m.

Choke 11 wins on raw resistance and loses on quality. Worth building if the only number that matters is worst-band R, and worth skipping otherwise.

What separates the winners is the high-end section. Both chokes that meet the full criterion use #43@5T. Every build whose high-end section carries six or eight turns gives up 10 m.

Re-dressing the leads moved the whole top of the range

Nothing below 20 m moved, and everything above it did. That is the signature of stray capacitance and nothing else: capacitance is invisible where the reactance it shunts is small, and it sets the ceiling where that reactance is large. The lead dressing was carrying capacitance across the winding, and taking it out raised the ceiling the top four bands were sitting under.

Two consequences for the build:

  • It is now the best choke here, 6,858 ohms on its worst band, against 6,706 for the same parts dressed loosely.
  • The limiting band moved from 10 m to 80 m. The choke is no longer short of impedance at the top; it is short at the bottom, where only more turns or more permeability will help. Its worst R/|Z| improved from 0.76 to 0.89.

This also measures the single-sleeve capacitance figures from the other direction. Those were taken with each sleeve’s loops standing proud and alligator clips centimeters apart, and the article calls them an upper bound. Re-dressing an assembly and watching only the top four bands move is the same statement made on a built choke: lead geometry, not the ferrite, sets what the high bands do.

The design rule the measurements give

Every choke here is limited at either 10 m or 160 m, and turns trade one against the other. The one-at-a-time builds measure the trade directly, each comparison being one choke against one choke:

  • Choke 8 to choke 9, high-end section #43@6T to #43@8T: 10 m rises from 4,378 to 6,282 ohms, and the limit moves to the low end.
  • Choke 10 to choke 11, #43@6T to #43@5T at nine turns of mix 75: 10 m rises from 4,256 to 7,369 ohms, and again the limit moves low.
  • Choke 7 to choke 8, replacing a #75@8T section with #31@6T: 10 m falls by 1,260 ohms. The mix-75 sections are load-bearing at the top of the range, not only at the bottom, which was not expected.
  • Choke 8 to choke 10, #75@8T to #75@9T: nothing gained at 10 m, while 30 m rises from 16,109 to 35,171 ohms. The ninth turn moves impedance to a band that already had a large surplus.

Turns raise low-frequency impedance and lower the section’s own resonance, so turns bought at the top are paid for at the bottom. A four-core choke covering 80 through 10 m wants its sections deliberately mismatched: many turns of high-permeability mix for the bottom, few turns of a lower-permeability mix for the top, and nothing in between trying to do both. That is what chokes 1 and 2 are.

Watch out. The #43@5T section both winners depend on is the one section never swept on its own, so this rule rests on the assemblies rather than on any characterization of the part. Each choke was swept once, with no repeats, so the scatter between two nominally identical windings is unknown and bounds how small a difference above is worth reading.

These are the most solid numbers in this work, because they reproduce to within 0.03 on a second cable measured the same week, with different turn counts and a different stray capacitance. Fair-Rite’s data is not wrong; it is measured on a specified toroid and does not transfer intact to a 26 mm sleeve.

Mix 75 cannot be characterized by either dataset. Every mix-75 winding, down to one turn, has its impedance maximum at or below the 1 MHz start of the sweep. The data cannot separate two readings: either the windings are past an LC resonance below 1 MHz, or mix 75’s loss permeability peaks below 1 MHz as a material property and the windings are fine. The second is physically expected for a high-permeability manganese-zinc ferrite, and a one-turn mix-75 sleeve calculates to resonate near 107 MHz. But the measured reactance crosses negative at 1.1 to 1.4 MHz, which is the signature of the first, and at 70 to 100 ohms that sign is fixture noise. A sweep starting at 100 kHz would settle it. Earlier passes assumed a flat 0.41 for mix 75 and every build they produced is withdrawn.

Watch out. Four tests for which turn counts to trust at each frequency failed before the pre-resonance rule. Largest |Z| in the sweep: one bad reference sample near 9.5 MHz spiked every device there, so the filter kept only the sweep whose calibration had failed. Inductive-only: admits mix 75’s post-resonance windings, inflating its loss permeability to 3.6 times published. Low-edge |Z| peak: cannot tell material relaxation from an LC resonance. First downward zero crossing of X: correct in principle, defeated by noise at 70 to 100 ohms.

Why the naive models did not work

Not because sections interact. That was the hypothesis the failures suggested, and it is wrong. Eleven assemblies built from nine distinct section types is an over-determined system, so one impedance can be solved for each section type and checked against every assembly at once. That solve closes:

Band 160 80 40 30 20 17 15 12 10
Residual 1% 3% 6% 8% 6% 6% 5% 6% 8%

Sections add in series, to within 1 to 8 percent. One consistent value per section reproduces eleven different chokes. Adjacent sleeves on a shared run are not coupling in any way that matters, and Z_total = ΣZ_section is sound.

It is the per-section impedances that are wrong, and they came from the bench. The single-sleeve sweeps are the only source of them, and they were taken at a looser lead dressing than any assembly: loops standing proud, clips centimeters apart, 289 nH of fixture residual against the assembly geometry’s

  1. That geometry adds capacitance, which drops the resonance and takes the middle and upper bands with it. So every capacitance figure derived from a single sleeve, 0.21 to 0.33 pF per turn, is an upper bound, and the assembled value is lower, in the direction of the commercial 0.15 to 0.28. Recalibrating the capacitance in either direction bottoms out at 23 percent mean error and never reproduces the shape: the model gives flat responses where the real chokes have strong mid-band peaks.

And the right per-section values cannot be recovered from these eleven chokes. Solving a section library from the assemblies fits them well and then fails to predict. Holding each choke out, fitting the rest, and predicting the one held out misses by 27 percent on average, from −60 to +59 percent, and several sections solve to negative resistance above 20 m. The cause is in the experiment rather than the arithmetic: #75@8T appears in six of the seven cleanest builds, so the columns are nearly collinear and any division of impedance between sections that sums correctly will fit. The sum is measured. The split is not.

Adding one end-to-end stray capacitance across the whole assembly removes the negative values at 0.75 to 1.0 pF for no loss of fit, which is a reason to prefer that small value on parsimony. It is not a measurement of one: the residual falls monotonically as that capacitance is raised with no interior minimum, by 5 pF the solved sections decay as 1/f, meaning the parameter is absorbing signal rather than explaining it, and out-of-sample error gets worse at 47 percent.

What would settle it is sweeping each candidate section alone, in the assembly fixture, at the assembly lead dressing. Five sweeps. Series summation is measured sound, so correct per-section impedances are the only missing ingredient, and the existing single-sleeve set cannot supply them.

What this does not establish

No stacked section has ever been swept. All eleven chokes are four separate single-core sections, so the Cp · N · √n_c stacking assumption is untested by every measurement here. Any modeled build containing a two-sleeve section rests on it.

Two of eleven is the hit rate on the full criterion, and both successes use a section never characterized alone.

One sweep per choke, no repeats, so the measurement scatter is unknown.

The 5-kilohm criterion is a target, not a measured requirement. Nothing here measures common-mode current on an actual antenna.

k rests on a resonance proxy, a smoothed impedance maximum rather than a measured resonance, and mix 75 is the case that proves the proxy can fail.

RG-316 caps the choke near a few hundred watts. It buys the turns these designs need at the cost of power handling, and nothing here weighs that trade.

What to build and measure

Design by model is finished until the inputs improve. The remaining measurements are the ones that would make a model possible, in the order they pay.

Sweep each candidate section alone, in the assembly fixture, at the assembly lead dressing. Five sweeps: #75@8T, #31@4T, #31@6T, #43@5T, #43@6T. Then predict a held-out choke and check. This is the one experiment that would turn a measured rule back into a design tool.

Wind the same section twice and sweep both. One sweep, and it bounds the scatter that every single-variable comparison above currently lacks. The re-dressed build is not that measurement: it changed the lead geometry deliberately, so it bounds nothing. Until this exists, read the +3 percent at 160 m and the −1 percent at 30 m in that comparison as noise rather than as small effects.

Put the turns back into the low end of the re-dressed build. Its weak band is now 80 m at 6,858 ohms, and nothing above 20 m is close. More turns on the mix-75 sections buy the bottom at the cost of the top, and the top now has 3,300 ohms of headroom to spend.

Wind #43@5T against #43@8T in one choke. Both winners use a low-turn high-end section, and the measurements say turns at the top are paid for at the bottom. A choke holding both extremes tests whether the trade can be split rather than chosen.

Sweep a stacked section. It is the largest untested assumption still in the model.

Mix 75 from 100 kHz. It now measures load-bearing at 10 m as well as at the bottom of the range, which no model expected.
