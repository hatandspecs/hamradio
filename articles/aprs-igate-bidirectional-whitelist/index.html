A Bidirectional APRS iGate with a Strict Whitelist

Published on: September 13, 2026  · 

An SMS from my phone, delivered over RF to my FT5DR: phone to the web to my gateway to RF to the radio

In my FT5DR article I mentioned wanting my own APRS igate with strictly whitelisted two-way traffic, so I could message my wife’s phone from a radio and she could message me back when I’m in range. That project for another day turned out to be only a few weeks out. This is how it came together: a prototype on the FTX-1 and a laptop, a headless Raspberry Pi I’m calling the pi-gate, a second radio profile for my VX-6R on a Digirig Lite, teaching the thing to find its own radio and put itself back on the air without me, and finally a mobile radio with enough power to be worth asking whether the regional digipeater can hear it. It also covers the handful of gotchas that each looked like a working station while doing nothing useful.

Code and full documentation are up at github.com/hatandspecs/aprs_igate_bidirectional_whitelist. The README has four quickstarts (laptop or Pi, FTX-1 or VX-6R), and there’s a separate step-by-step guide for building the pi-gate.

Why a whitelist, and why it has to be strict

Most APRS iGates are receive-only. They listen on 144.390 MHz and pass what they hear up to APRS-IS, the internet backbone that sites like aprs.fi are built on. Going the other direction, from the internet back onto RF, is where it gets touchy. A two-way iGate is a transmitter that traffic from the internet can key up, and I didn’t want my station relaying anything for anyone I hadn’t chosen.

So the rule I built around is short: only APRS messages addressed to callsigns on my whitelist are ever transmitted. Positions, telemetry, bulletins, and messages to anyone else are all dropped. Right now the list is KD3CCO*, which covers every SSID of my own call.

Here’s the round trip it enables, in both directions:

How the whitelist actually works

The gateway itself is Dire Wolf, the software TNC. My scripts generate its configuration from a much simpler config file. Four directives do the work that matters:

IGFILTER  g/KD3CCO*
FILTER    IG 0 g/KD3CCO*
IGMSP     0
IGTXLIMIT 6 10
  • FILTER IG 0 g/KD3CCO* is the whitelist itself. The g/ filter matches on a message’s addressee, which is what makes this a message whitelist rather than a general traffic filter. Anything from APRS-IS that doesn’t match never reaches the transmitter.
  • IGFILTER asks the APRS-IS server to send me that traffic in the first place. Without it, the server only forwards traffic for stations my iGate has recently heard on RF, which is nothing until something has been decoded.
  • IGMSP 0 turned out to matter a lot. Dire Wolf has a courtesy feature that transmits a message sender’s position report, regardless of filtering, after it gates their message. I watched it transmit the SMS gateway’s own beacon right after delivering my message. IGMSP 0 turns that off, so the whitelist has no exceptions, courtesy or otherwise.
  • IGTXLIMIT 6 10 caps traffic from the internet at 6 transmissions a minute and 10 every five minutes, as a backstop. Anything over the limit is dropped, not queued for later.

This is the decision every packet arriving from APRS-IS goes through:

flowchart TD
  A["Packet arrives from APRS-IS"] --> B{"Is it an APRS message?"}
  B -- no --> D["Dropped"]
  B -- yes --> C{"Addressee on the whitelist?"}
  C -- no --> D
  C -- yes --> E["Wrapped in third-party format,<br/>rate limited, transmitted on RF"]

Log lines don’t mean what they look like

This one cost me some confusion. Dire Wolf prints [ig>tx] when a packet arrives from APRS-IS, before the whitelist has run. The line that means it was actually transmitted is [0L]. Read [ig>tx] as “transmitted” and a perfectly working whitelist looks badly broken.

So I wrote an annotating monitor that pairs the two by packet contents and labels the real outcome:

Label Meaning
RF RX Heard on the air and decoded
RF->IS UP Heard on RF and gated up to APRS-IS by this station
IS GATED Came from APRS-IS, matched the whitelist, was transmitted
IS DROP Came from APRS-IS, didn’t match, dropped
TX LOCAL This station’s own beacon

A dropped packet can only be known by the absence of a transmission, so IS DROP shows up about 15 seconds after the packet arrives, once it’s clearly not just waiting its turn to transmit.

The prototype: FTX-1 and a laptop

I proved everything out on the bench first, with the FTX-1 Optima on a tripod and Dire Wolf running in a locked-down Docker container on my laptop.

The prototype: FTX-1 Optima at 144.390 and the laptop running the gateway in Docker

The FTX-1’s single USB-C cable exposes CAT control and PTT as two separate serial ports, and Dire Wolf’s rig-control PTT only takes one. rigctld from Hamlib bridges them, using the FT-991 model (1035), which works for the FTX-1. Dire Wolf then keys the radio through rigctld over loopback.

The container is deliberately cramped: all Linux capabilities dropped, read-only root filesystem, non-root user, only the radio’s own device nodes (not the laptop’s built-in microphone), and outbound traffic filtered down to DNS and the APRS-IS port. Dire Wolf’s AGW and KISS network ports are disabled too. It binds them to every interface with no authentication, so anything on the network could have transmitted under my call.

The first message from my phone to the FT5DR through this chain was a genuinely good moment. That’s it at the top of this article, sent from my phone to the web, to the FTX-1 and laptop, and over RF to the FT5DR.

Three things that looked fine and weren’t

Plain FM instead of D-FM. In plain FM, the FTX-1 modulates from the microphone input, not the USB audio. Dire Wolf keyed the radio, a clean carrier went out, SWR was fine, the waterfall showed a signal, and nothing on earth could decode it. The data mode is D-FM (Hamlib calls it PKTFM). The startup script now sets frequency and mode over CAT on every start, and warns if the radio reports plain FM.

Audio levels. The USB codec’s default transmit level over-deviates, again audible but undecodable, and the default capture gain is far too low. Levels reset whenever the radio’s USB re-enumerates, so the script sets them on every start.

RF on the USB cable. At 5 W on a quarter-wave whip with no ground plane, RF crashed the USB link mid-transmission, twice, and left the radio stuck keyed with nothing able to unkey it. The fix was a properly matched half-wave antenna on a tripod, plus a ferrite choke on the USB cable. That’s the red cable wound around the green toroid in these photos. I also set the radio’s time-out timer to 3 minutes, since that’s the only thing that can end a stuck transmission once the control path itself has died.

The pi-gate

A laptop tied up running an iGate isn’t a permanent solution, so the gateway moved to a Raspberry Pi. It started on a 3A+ — 512 MB of RAM, one USB port, no Ethernet, and small enough to forget about — and now runs on a 3B+, for reasons that come up later: the 3A+’s single USB port is wired straight to the processor, and the Digirig wants a hub in front of it. The 3B+ has one on the board. Everything else about the build is identical, and the same image script produces a card for either.

The Raspberry Pi 3A+ with the radio’s USB cable through a ferrite choke

I didn’t want to hand-configure a Pi over a keyboard and monitor, so a script builds the SD card image on my laptop. It downloads Raspberry Pi OS Lite, loop-mounts the image, and writes everything in: WiFi profiles, SSH, the user account, the project, and systemd services. The Pi boots, joins WiFi, installs Dire Wolf, and starts gating with no console session at any point.

  B --> C["First boot: joins WiFi,<br/>installs Dire Wolf"]
  C --> D["Gateway service runs 'up'"]
  D --> E{"Radio's USB devices present?"}
  E -- "yes" --> G["Set audio levels, start Dire Wolf"]

  R --> D
  G --> H["On the air, web monitor on port 8080"]

That wait-and-retry loop was added after real use. A radio plugged in or switched on after the Pi now brings the gateway up by itself.

Built to be unplugged

A box like this gets pulled from the wall rather than shut down, and an SD card being written when power disappears is how Pis get corrupted. So the image removes the routine writers:

  • The runtime directory (logs, generated config, pid files) lives in RAM.
  • The packet log is rotated hourly, so it can’t fill that space.
  • The system journal is kept in RAM.
  • Swap is compressed RAM only. Raspberry Pi OS’s default also writes idle pages out to a swap file on the card, which I didn’t catch until I read the Pi’s own boot log.

What’s left is a card that only gets written when I deliberately change something.

One config, layered

The same station configuration runs on the laptop and on the Pi, so settings are split by what they actually describe:

flowchart BT
  P["Radio profile<br/>how to drive the FTX-1 or VX-6R"] --> S["igate.conf<br/>the station: callsign, whitelist, beacon"]
  S --> L["igate.local.conf<br/>this machine: Docker or bare metal, device paths"]
  L --> X["igate.secrets<br/>APRS-IS passcode"]

Each layer overrides the one below it. One restriction keeps this safe: the whitelist, callsign, beacon and transmit path can only come from igate.conf. A radio profile or a machine’s local file that tries to set them is refused. Which radio or which machine runs the gateway can never change what it transmits.

A second radio: the VX-6R on a Digirig Lite

The FTX-1 is too nice a radio to leave parked on 144.390, so I added a profile for my VX-6R on a Digirig Lite. It’s a very different arrangement:

  • No CAT. The VX-6R has no computer control, so frequency is set by hand and nothing in software can check it. The gateway prints a reminder on every start instead.
  • PTT through the sound card. The Digirig Lite is a CM108 USB sound card whose GPIO3 pin keys the radio through Digirig’s VX-6R cable. Dire Wolf drives that pin directly through the chip’s /dev/hidraw device.
  • Finding the right device. The FTX-1’s built-in codec is also a C-Media chip. So the script doesn’t take the first C-Media device it finds: it finds the one on the same USB device as the radio’s audio card, so a machine with both radios attached keys the right one.

From the VX-6R manual: set Receive Battery Saver (Set Mode 53) off, because its sleep cycle clips the start of incoming packets. Also turn auto power-off off and keep the time-out timer on. At the Digirig’s documented 50% starting levels, it carried full SMS round trips in both directions.

What the Digirig taught me

This part took the most troubleshooting, and each problem looked like a different one:

  1. The Pi 3A+ couldn’t run the Digirig directly. Its single USB port connects straight to the processor with no hub chip, and the Digirig simply never showed up. Through an unpowered hub the Pi logged under-voltage. Through a powered hub, it worked. This is what eventually moved the station to a 3B+, which has a hub chip on the board and takes the Digirig in any of its four ports with nothing in between.
  2. Don’t power the radio from the same hub. I’d powered the VX-6R through a USB-to-barrel cable with a 12 V boost converter, plugged into that hub. On the gateway’s first transmission, the radio’s current draw pulled the hub’s supply down and knocked the Digirig off USB. The Pi itself reported no under-voltage, because it regulates its own rail. The radio now has its own wall adapter.
  3. My Digirig’s USB-C connection only works one way round. Plugged in upside down, it was electrically invisible, with no error and nothing in the kernel log, on the Pi and on the laptop alike. Every replug was a coin toss, which made it look like an intermittent fault for a while. The working orientation is now marked.
  4. Card numbers weren’t stable. The Pi’s HDMI audio registered as sound card 1 at boot, so a Digirig plugged in afterwards became card 2. The image now turns HDMI audio off, so the radio is always card 1 — and, as of the rework below, the gateway no longer cares what number it gets.

More power: the FT-2900R, and a profile named for the interface

A handheld in a charger cradle is a fine gateway radio right up to the point where you want it heard somewhere. The regional digipeater, W3YA-1, sits 10.3 km from my house on Pine Grove Mountain — 49 watts into an 8 dBi omni at 640 feet above average terrain, according to its own PHG beacon. Reaching it turns my gateway from a station my own radios can use into one that is part of the network. So I moved the pi-gate onto a Yaesu FT-2900R, a 75 watt mobile, on a bench supply with a vertical on a camera tripod.

The FT-2900R pi-gate: mobile radio on a bench supply, Digirig Lite at right, antenna on a tripod

Adding it needed a third radio profile, and writing that profile is what showed me the first two were wrong. I put radtel880g.conf next to vx6r.conf for a Radtel 880G, then diffed them: they were identical except for the description line. Every setting in a Digirig profile describes the Digirig — its C-Media codec is the sound card, its GPIO3 is the PTT line, its mixer controls have fixed names. What the radio contributes is a speaker jack, a microphone jack and a volume knob.

So the two profiles became one, digirig.conf, named for the interface instead of the radio, and the FT-2900R joined it without a setting changing. The things that genuinely are per-radio — the frequency, the volume knob, the battery saver menu number, the transmit time-out, and now the power step — are all things no profile can set anyway, because there is no CAT port on any of them. The profile lists them as what the operator has to do, which is more honest than pretending a file controls them.

The cable that keyed the radio by itself

The FT-2900R’s first night on the air was the best gotcha of the whole project, because every symptom pointed away from the cause.

The gateway received perfectly. It decoded the digipeater, gated other people’s packets up to APRS-IS, and the monitor showed my own messages being transmitted. Dire Wolf logged [ig>tx] and then [0L] for each one, which is it saying the frame went to the modem. Nothing ever reached the air. Three beacons addressed through W3YA-1 produced no response, and every copy of my station on aprs.fi had arrived over the internet.

The tell was a light. With the Pi’s gateway stopped and nothing running at all, the Digirig’s PTT LED was on. Software cannot do that; PTT is a contact on the jack. I had the adapter cable in backwards — the TRS end in the Digirig’s TRRS socket, the TRRS end in the radio’s speaker jack. A three-conductor plug in a four-conductor socket bridges the second ring onto the sleeve, and on the Digirig that ring is PTT. It grounded the instant the plug seated.

What follows from that is the part worth remembering. The radio keyed an unmodulated carrier, its time-out timer cut transmit off, and because PTT was never released it stayed in transmit-inhibit from then on. Receive was unaffected, because receive arrives on a different conductor. The result was a station that looked healthy in every log I had and transmitted nothing, for as long as it took me to look at a light instead of at a log. The cable will seat either way round and both ways feel identical.

Asking a digipeater whether it can hear me

Turning the cable round fixed it, and the next question was the one I had built the radio up to ask. Confirming it needs more care than it sounds, because the obvious tests all answer something else.

Sending an SMS to my handheld proves nothing: the handheld is in the same house and hears the gateway directly whether or not a digipeater is involved. Watching for my beacon on aprs.fi proves nothing either, for a subtler reason. My beacon was set to BOTH, which sends a copy over the internet as well as over the air. APRS-IS discards duplicate packets within about thirty seconds, matching on source and payload while ignoring the path — so the internet copy, which arrives in milliseconds, masks the radio copy that took a digipeater to get there. The evidence destroys itself.

The test config that does answer it changes four settings:

TX_VIA       = W3YA-1     name the digipeater instead of an alias
RX_VIA       = W3YA-1     gate only packets that digipeater actually repeated
BEACON_TO    = RF         the beacon is the test transmission
BEACON_EVERY = 2:00       ask often enough to get an answer

Naming the digipeater rather than WIDE1-1 removes the question of which aliases it answers: a digipeater repeats any packet carrying its own callsign, whatever else it is configured for. RX_VIA leans on the AX.25 has-been-repeated bit, so it means genuinely relayed by that station and not merely addressed to it. And RF rather than BOTH is the whole point — with it, the only way that beacon can reach aprs.fi is by radio.

Two minutes later the monitor had the answer, and it needed nobody else’s station to confirm it:

17:01:03  TX LOCAL   KD3CCO-10>APDW17,W3YA-1:!4046.25NR07747.50W&RX iGate | TX whitelist only
17:01:05  RF RX      KD3CCO-10>APDW17,W3YA-1*:!4046.25NR07747.50W&RX iGate | TX whitelist only

The same packet, two seconds apart, with the asterisk moved onto W3YA-1. I transmitted it, the digipeater repeated it, and my own receiver decoded the repeat.

It is a marginal path, though. Over eight beacons, two came back. I raised the radio two power steps partway through and it changed nothing I can defend as a result — two of three before, none of five after, which sounds decisive and isn’t: at that sample size the difference is indistinguishable from chance. Eight decibels bought no measurable improvement — and the reason, which took another day and a different fault to find, is that the antenna was not working.

It was a dual-band mobile whip on a camera tripod in my living room. A mobile whip is designed to sit on a car roof, and the roof is the other half of it; without a ground plane the coax braid becomes the counterpoise, and a good deal of what I was radiating was coming off the feedline. More power into an antenna that is not one buys nothing, which is exactly the result I measured and exactly the wrong conclusion I drew from it.

Obstruction was never the problem. I work the local FM repeater from inside this house on five watts and a handheld whip; the path out is fine. The two-in-eight is a real number about that tripod arrangement and nothing else, and it needs measuring again now the antenna is outside.

The measurement that did mean something came from a station I never asked. Every one of those eight beacons reached aprs.fi, gated a second after transmission by W3SWL-2, a receive-only gate that heard all eight at both power settings. That rules out a lot at once: a distorted signal from RF getting into the audio would have failed there too, and so would a radio folding its power back into a bad match. The transmitter is clean, and it is one particular path that is marginal. My gateway was already on APRS-IS by radio through a neighbor the whole time — W3YA-1 was never a requirement for the station to be part of the network, only the question I happened to be asking.

If I run this test again I will change the beacon between transmissions. Mine was byte-identical every two minutes, and digipeaters suppress duplicates — which does not explain this particular pattern, but it does make an unchanging position beacon a poor instrument for asking the question.

One last trap, which cost me a wrong conclusion before I read my own log properly. aprs.fi showed that same beacon as KD3CCO-10>APDW17,W3YA-1,qAO,W3SWL-2, with no asterisk — apparently proof the digipeater had not repeated it. That entry is a different copy of the same transmission: a neighboring receive-only gate heard me directly, gated it a second earlier, and won the duplicate race. My own gateway’s copy of the repeat arrived afterwards and was discarded. What you see on aprs.fi is whichever copy got there first, which may have taken a different route than the one you heard yourself. For what your own station heard, the only authority is your own monitor.

RX_VIA is deliberately antisocial — while it is set, everything that did not come through that one digipeater goes ungated — so it belongs in a separate config file that is never the one running by default, and it goes back off as soon as it has answered.

Making it a box you can just power on

Two things still needed a human, and both of them produced a station that looked fine.

The first was the sound card’s number. The Digirig shows up as an ALSA card, and which number it gets depends on which USB port it’s in and what else enumerated first. The config named that number, so moving the Digirig to a different port meant editing a file.

The second was worse. Every so often the kernel would reset the sound card in place — usb 1-1.1.3: reset full-speed USB device number 4. RF getting into the USB cable does this, and it happened most often right after the gateway transmitted. A reset leaves the device present: lsusb still lists it, arecord -l still shows the card, the gateway still reports itself as running. But the handle Dire Wolf opened at startup is dead, and it says so, forever:

Audio input device 0 error code -19: No such device

Nothing is gated from that moment until someone notices and restarts the service. Systemd wasn’t going to help: the unit is a oneshot that launches Dire Wolf and returns, so once a start has succeeded, systemd considers the job done.

Finding the radio by what it is. The radio profile stopped naming a card number:

ADEVICE = auto
USB_ID = 0d8c:0012

0d8c:0012 is the C-Media chip as lsusb prints it. The gateway searches for the sound card belonging to that USB device — at every start, and again on every retry while it’s waiting for the radio to appear. Any port, any card number, plugged in before or after boot. PTT comes along for free, because the /dev/hidraw node was already being found on the audio card’s own USB device. If two cards match the same id it refuses rather than guessing; guessing means keying the wrong radio.

A watchdog for everything after startup. A systemd timer runs a check every minute, and a udev rule runs the same check the moment a USB sound card appears, so a replug is caught in seconds rather than at the next tick.

flowchart TD
  A["Check runs: timer, or udev on plug-in"] --> B{"Gateway ever started here?"}
  B -- "no" --> Z["Do nothing"]
  B -- "yes" --> C{"Radio present?"}
  C -- "no" --> W["Say so once, wait.<br/>Restarting would only<br/>time out"]
  C -- "yes" --> D{"Dire Wolf running?"}
  D -- "no" --> R["Restart"]
  D -- "yes" --> E{"Radio on a different<br/>ALSA card now?"}
  E -- "yes" --> R
  E -- "no" --> F{"New '-19' errors<br/>since last check?"}
  F -- "yes" --> R
  F -- "no" --> Z

That last branch is the one that catches the reset, because it’s the only signal that disagrees: the process is alive, the device is listed, and the card number hasn’t moved. Restarts are limited to one every three minutes so a genuinely broken radio can’t put the station in a loop — except that the limit is cleared whenever the radio goes missing, since a radio that’s come back isn’t a loop, it’s a new situation. I got that wrong first time round and watched a replug sit waiting out a limit set by something that happened before I’d unplugged anything.

And one more thing that looked right and wasn’t. The udev rule didn’t fire. It matched ENV{ID_BUS}=="usb", and udevadm info cheerfully reports ID_BUS=usb on the card — but udevadm test, which replays the rules, shows that property absent from the set the rules are evaluated against. The database has it; the rules never see it. The filter was only there to avoid waking the check for a non-radio sound card, and the check costs nothing and decides for itself anyway, so I dropped it.

On the bench it now does what it says. A reset at 20:19:34 had the gateway restarted 18 seconds later with no input from me. Unplugging the Digirig and putting it back in a different USB port — a different branch of the Pi’s internal hub — produced a watchdog run six seconds after the plug went in, off the timer’s cadence, and the gateway came back on the re-resolved card.

None of which fixes the actual fault. Four resets in one evening is RF getting somewhere it shouldn’t, and the honest repair is ferrites and distance between the antenna and the Pi, not a watchdog that tidies up afterwards. But an unattended box should put itself back on the air, and now it does.

One more gotcha: third-party messages

I added a friend to the whitelist, and his station never acknowledged a single message, even though my gateway transmitted every retry at 5 W on the half-wave antenna. The cause was his APRS app.

My gateway can’t transmit a message as if it came from the SMS gateway, since that’s not my station’s callsign. So Dire Wolf wraps every message it gates in APRS third-party format:

KD3CCO-10>APDW17:}SMS>APOSMS,TCPIP,KD3CCO-10*::KD3CCO-7 :@XXXXXXXXXX phone to pigate test{16565

Everything after the } is the original packet. My FT5DR unwraps it, sees a message for KD3CCO-7, and acks within seconds. His Dire Wolf decoded the packet fine, but the app on top of it ignored third-party packets. It never saw a message addressed to him, so it never acked, and the SMS gateway kept retrying. The RF path was working; the software wasn’t. The fix is on the receiving end: an app that handles third-party packets.

Where it stands

The pi-gate runs unattended on a Pi 3B+ — on the FT-2900R now, with the VX-6R and the Radtel as drop-in alternatives on the same Digirig profile — beacons every 30 minutes as an R-overlay iGate (“RX iGate, TX whitelist only”), carries messages to and from my radios in both directions, and reaches APRS-IS over the air rather than only over the internet. It finds its radio by USB id rather than by port, and puts itself back on the air after a replug or a USB reset without being asked. The beacon position is the center of my 6-character grid square rather than my house, which keeps it a couple of kilometers from exact.

What I have actually proved, and what I haven’t

The point of all this is a specific thing: my wife texts my phone number from her phone, and it comes out of a radio in my pocket somewhere I have no cell coverage, and I can answer her. It is worth being precise about which parts of that are demonstrated and which are still assumption.

Proved. The whitelist holds, and the gateway transmits nothing else. Both directions of the SMS round trip work with the handheld in the house. The gateway reaches APRS-IS over the air rather than only over the internet — seventeen beacons out of seventeen, every one of them gated by a neighboring receive-only station without a single gap. And crucially, the gateway will transmit a message to my handheld whether or not it has heard my handheld, because the explicit IS-to-RF filter replaces Dire Wolf’s default of only messaging stations heard nearby recently. That one is a design decision rather than a measurement, but it is the decision the whole idea rests on: if my gateway only talked to radios already in its own range, extending that range would be pointless.

Not proved. The case that matters. Everything above is a house full of radios talking to each other.

I have come close once. With the VX-6R as the gateway radio I had reliable two-way messaging from South Hills Business School, across town, where the Nittany Amateur Radio Club holds its monthly meetings — much nearer the real thing than anything I did tonight. There is a neatness to that: W3YA-1 is NARC’s own digipeater, so whichever way the messages traveled, the club was involved. What I failed to do was record the path, and without it that success is ambiguous in precisely the way that matters. If a digipeater relayed those messages, the case is made. If my handheld was still inside my own gateway’s footprint, it was a range test rather than a relay test, and the capability I am trying to build remains untested. Messages arriving looks identical either way, which is the whole problem.

There is a clue buried in it, though. That was five watts from a handheld sitting in a charger cradle. The mobile radio on a tripod, at six times the power, gets repeated by the digipeater one time in four. If the handheld’s antenna happened to be somewhere better in the house, that says more about what is wrong with the current arrangement than any amount of power ever will.

The one link standing between the two is the one I measured: a digipeater repeated my gateway twice in nine. That is not a delivery path yet.

There is an irony in those two numbers sitting next to each other. The station that hears me perfectly is receive-only, so it can never send anything back; the station that could send something back hears me one time in four. My reach is excellent on the leg that was never the problem.

Why 30 watts inside a house loses to 5 watts on a handheld

What confused me is that I work the local FM repeater easily from inside the house, with a whip on a handheld at 5 watts. So why does a 75 watt mobile struggle to be heard by a digipeater 10 km away?

Path loss is reciprocal, and that turns out to be the whole story. My gateway decodes W3YA-1’s repeats all evening. The path carries its signal to me perfectly well. What differs is the two ends: it runs 49 watts into an 8 dBi omni at 640 feet above average terrain, and I run 30 watts into a Diamond SG7500NMO — a dual-band mobile whip, on an NMO mount on a camera tripod in my living room. That is ten to twenty decibels of asymmetry, almost all of it antenna and surroundings. A link can be comfortably one-way, and mine is.

The mode makes it worse in ways voice never shows you:

  • A packet is all or nothing. 1200 baud AX.25 has no forward error correction. One corrupted bit fails the CRC and the whole frame is discarded. There is no mostly-decoded packet. Voice stays useful through hiss, flutter and picket-fencing because my ears do the error correction; the modem has none.
  • It has to hold for the whole burst. A voice transmission is seconds long and only the syllables need to survive. A beacon is one burst of about half a second. Indoors, multipath puts deep nulls in the room that move as people do. Voice rides through them. A packet that meets one dies completely, which is exactly what an intermittent result with no dependence on power looks like.
  • Collisions are invisible and immune to power. 144.390 is a single shared national channel carrying every APRS station for a hundred miles. If anything else arrives at the digipeater while my burst does, FM capture takes the stronger one. I see my transmission go out and nothing come back, which looks identical to being too weak. On a voice repeater I listen before keying and hear immediately when I did not make it.
  • The comparison was never like for like. The FM repeater I can work is not that digipeater. Different site, different height, different receiver.

What it is not is my audio levels, and that is worth stating because it was my first suspicion. The same transmissions were decoded seventeen times out of seventeen by another station. The signal leaving the radio is fine.

So the fix is not watts. Eight decibels of extra power bought nothing measurable, because the limiting item was the antenna rather than the path: an N9TAX slim jim is a self-contained end-fed half-wave whose matching stub does the job the missing ground plane was doing, so it does not need the feedline as a counterpoise. Hung outside, it fixes the asymmetry, the multipath and the stray RF in one move.

The test that would settle it

Three stages, and only the last one needs a car.

  1. Measure the real downlink reach from home. Beacon with a generic WIDE1-1 path rather than naming one digipeater, and tally which callsigns come back with the has-been-repeated bit set, and how often. Naming a digipeater answers “can that one hear me”; coverage is a question about all of them, and my logs are full of digipeaters I have never once asked. This is a measured map of where a message to me could land, obtained without leaving the house.
  2. Fix the antenna, then measure again. Same test, so the numbers are comparable.
  3. Go somewhere out of my own range. Qualify the location first, or the result means nothing: at the spot, my handheld should decode the digipeater’s beacons and should not decode my gateway directly. Then have a message sent from a phone, and check the path in the frame that arrives, not merely that something arrived — my gateway’s callsign has to be in it, or some other transmitting iGate did the work and I have learned nothing about mine. Then reply, and confirm the reply lands. Several times, because a single success proves nothing about a path currently running at one in four.

One thing works in my favor throughout. Both ends retry: I watched the handheld resend an unacknowledged message four times over four minutes, and the SMS gateway resend one after seventy-five seconds. A link that gets through one time in four still delivers, it just takes a few minutes and costs airtime. Marginal and useless are not the same thing here. The acknowledgement coming back is the real success criterion, because it is the only event that proves both directions completed.

The station that restarted every time it transmitted

The morning after all that, the gateway was flaky in a way I could not put my finger on. The web monitor was frozen at a timestamp eight hours old while aprs.fi showed the station beaconing happily right up to the present minute.

Those are two different things being watched, which is the first thing worth saying. The beacons on aprs.fi were all qAS — the internet copy, sent over TCP. They proved Dire Wolf was alive and said nothing whatever about the radio, or about the page. The page is a separate program reading the log. A frozen page is not evidence about the gateway, and I spent a few minutes treating it as though it were.

The real fault was in the beacon timestamps, and it took reading them as a sequence rather than a list. Every so often there was an extra beacon about a minute after a scheduled one, and from that point the 30-minute schedule ran from the new time. That is what a restart looks like from outside: Dire Wolf beacons shortly after it starts. Counting them gave seven restarts in eight hours.

The monitor had caught one in the act:

00:14:37  TX LOCAL   KD3CCO-10>APDW17,WIDE1-1:...
00:14:38  WARN       Audio input device 0 error code -19: No such device
00:14:46  INFO       Now connected to IGate server noam.aprs2.net

Error −19 is ENODEV. The sound card had not gone quiet or misbehaved — it had left the bus. One second after transmitting.

The kernel log settled it:

[Sat Sep 26 00:14:38] usb 1-1.1.3: reset full-speed USB device number 4 using dwc_otg

Eight of those, and every one within a second or two of a transmission, with none in between. Two details in that line matter more than they look:

  • reset, not disconnect, and the same device number throughout. A disconnect means the thing lost power or fell out of its socket. A reset means the kernel saw errors on a link that never left the bus — which is RF corrupting USB traffic, not a connection problem.
  • vcgencmd get_throttled returned 0x0. No under-voltage, not then and not latched since boot. The Pi browning out under transmit current produces an identical-looking log and needs the opposite fix, so that was one command well spent.

I nearly drew a wrong conclusion here. The first reset in the log was 19:43 on the Friday, which is about when the mobile radio went into service — tidy evidence that the previous handheld never did this. Except dmesg only covers the current boot, and the Pi had booted at 19:42:36. The first reset was 57 seconds after power-on, on Dire Wolf’s own startup beacon. I had read an empty buffer as an absence of events. The journal is Storage=volatile on this image to spare the SD card, so the older history is genuinely gone and that question stays open.

What was actually wrong was the antenna, not the power. A dual-band mobile whip is designed to sit on a car roof, and the roof is the other half of it. On a camera tripod in a living room that half is missing, so the coax braid becomes the counterpoise — common-mode current is not a side effect there, it is structural. The strongest RF in the room was running along the feedline, past the Pi.

The fix was three things at once: a common-mode choke at the feedpoint, more ferrites on the interface and power cables, and the mobile whip replaced with a slim jim hung outside several meters from everything. A slim jim is a self-contained end-fed half-wave — the matching stub does the job the missing ground plane was doing, so it does not need the feedline as a counterpoise.

It is hung rather than mounted — a monoband N9TAX I already had, after buying the dual-band version for the FTX-1 in the shack. For now it dangles from the porch roof with the coax running back inside. It belongs up high on the house and will get there, but even from the porch it was enough.

The N9TAX slim jim hanging from the porch roof, coax running back to the radio — temporary, and still the single biggest improvement in this project

Then the honest test: put the radio on full power, the setting that had been causing a reset roughly every other transmission, and see if it falls over. Eight transmissions later — a beacon, four gated messages, and the acknowledgements coming back — the reset count had not moved. At the old rate, eight clean transmissions is about a one percent coincidence.

Backing the power off would have hidden this and I would have called it fixed. It is worth being clear that lower power was never the cure, only a way to stop noticing.

Two weeks later: the measurement I said I needed

The antenna is outside now, and the number I kept saying needed retaking got retaken. I built it into the tool rather than doing it by hand, because the measurement turns out to be free: every beacon this station has ever sent was an experiment that already ran, and the answers are sitting in the log. A repeat of my own beacon arrives over the air like any other frame, carrying the callsign of whatever repeated it and the has-been-repeated bit set. ./deploy_igate.sh reach counts them. It transmits nothing.

  transmitted by KD3CCO-10:       112
  heard back, digipeated:       42
  repeat rate:                  37%

Heard directly by:
  W3TM-10      28
  W3YA-1       14

Two things in that need saying, and the second one embarrassed me.

The first is that I had been asking the wrong question. Everything above in this article is me asking “can W3YA-1 hear my gateway,” because W3YA-1 is the digipeater on the mountain and the one I knew about. Over 112 beacons the answer is about one in eight. Resist reading that as a decline from the two-in-nine I measured off the tripod, tempting as it is — two successes out of nine attempts is consistent with a true rate anywhere from a few percent to better than half, so there is nothing there to have declined from. It was never a number, it was a rumor. This one is a number. But “can W3YA-1 hear me” was never the question. The question is whether a message can get to me, and the answer to that is 37%, because W3TM-10 — a station a couple of miles from here that I had never once asked about — was quietly doing two thirds of the work the whole time. My logs were full of digipeaters I had never thought to count.

The second is that the obvious fix was backwards. My instinct, and the advice earlier in this article, was to name W3YA-1 explicitly in the transmit path: a digipeater repeats any frame carrying its own callsign, so naming it removes all the guesswork about which aliases it answers. That reasoning is still correct and the conclusion is still wrong. Those counts are not a race. With one hop on offer, every digipeater that hears the original repeats it — they don’t coordinate — so 28 and 14 are two independent hearing rates, not a contest that W3TM-10 won. Naming W3YA-1 would have thrown away the station that hears me most often in order to force the one that hears me least. I would have made my gateway quieter and called it an improvement.

What actually reaches the mountain is a second slot in the path. W3TM-10 hears the original and repeats it; a wide-area hop written after the first one survives that repeat; W3YA-1 picks it up off W3TM-10’s transmitter instead of mine, which is the one that is fighting this terrain. Both digipeaters get used, in the order that plays to what each is good at, and nothing is given up to get there.

So the transmit path is now WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2 — more hops than an iGate is generally supposed to use, and I think defensible here. Phone-to-handheld traffic on this station is a few messages a month. Three hops on a message that rare is a rounding error next to one mobile running SmartBeacon down the interstate.

That argument only holds because I split a setting that should never have been one setting. The same value was being applied to gated messages and to the beacon, and those differ in volume by two orders of magnitude: a handful of messages a month against roughly fourteen hundred beacons. Reach that is cheap on a message is not cheap fourteen hundred times. Dire Wolf had always kept them apart — IGTXVIA for gated traffic, the beacon’s own via= for the beacon — and it was my config layer that welded them together. There are two settings now, and the beacon stayed at one hop.

The bug that was waiting for me to need it

Writing a two-element path for the first time turned up something that had been broken since the beginning.

Dire Wolf splits a configuration line on whitespace. My renderer normalized paths to spaces. So IGTXVIA 0 WIDE1-1 WIDE2-2 is not a two-hop path — it is the path WIDE1-1, followed by a token the parser never looks at. Every message would have gone out one hop short of where I aimed it, every log line would have said it was transmitted, and nothing anywhere would have raised an error.

It was invisible for as long as the path had one element, which was the entire life of the project until the day I needed two. I did not find it because something failed. I found it because I had got into the habit of reading the rendered run/direwolf.conf after changing anything, which is a habit this project has beaten into me. The list of things in this article that were wrong while every log said they were fine is now long enough that I read the generated file on principle.

It tests itself now

The other thing that changed is that the gateway no longer waits for me to notice. Every four hours it injects a real message to my handheld from a second callsign of my own, then reads its own log for four separate events: APRS-IS accepted it, the server routed it back here, the gateway transmitted it, and my handheld acknowledged it over the air. It reports which of the four failed.

A beacon proves the transmitter keys. Only an acknowledgement proves the station can do the thing it exists for — and this gateway had already spent hours beaconing cheerfully while unable to deliver anything at all, which is the whole reason the check exists.

It has caught one real failure so far, and I want to record it because my first instinct was to soften the test. The self-test failed at step 4: transmitted, never acknowledged. I assumed the handheld was off. It was not off. It was sitting on the calling frequency instead of 144.390, because I had been using it for something else and put it down without thinking. The test’s own failure message said to check that the radio was on and on 144.390, and it was right, and I nearly taught it to stay quiet in exactly the case it was built for.

The journal is on disk now too, capped at 32 MB. It lives in RAM by default to spare the SD card, which is the right call right up until the morning you want to know what happened before the last reboot — and I have had that morning. A bounded write cost is worth paying to stop the machine erasing its own evidence.

The step that isn’t in this article, and produces silence when you miss it

Something I glossed over the first time through, because it worked for me early and I never had to think about it again: the phone number on the far end has to be opted in before the SMS bridge will deliver anything to it. That requirement comes from the carriers, not from APRS.

It deserves a place in a piece that is mostly about failures that look like other failures, because this is one of them. A message to a number that has not opted in is not delivered and nothing reports an error — not the bridge, not my gateway, not the monitor. It leaves correctly, it is never answered, and that is precisely what a dead transmitter looks like. Or a radio on the wrong frequency. Or a path that does not close. I spent a day on the last of those; I would have spent a week on this one.

It is also worth naming the service, which the article above never does. The bridge I use, and have used throughout this project, is run by NA7Q and documented at aprs.wiki. It answers to the callsign SMS on the air and to 866-352-4096 from a phone.

Worth knowing if you go looking for guidance: most of what you will find online describes SMSGTE, an earlier service that shut down over spam and carrier-regulation trouble. Its domain has since lapsed and now serves an online casino, and a great deal of published material still links there.

Opting a number in is a web form on the front page of aprs.wiki. One field: the phone number, 10 digits for the US, Puerto Rico and Canada, 11 for Australia, 12 for the UK, with no leading 1 and no punctuation. Press Opt-In. Do it for every phone that will exchange messages with a radio. There is an Opt-Out box beside it, and texting STOP from the phone works at any time.

The opt-in form on aprs.wiki, with the opt-out box directly beneath it

Then there are aliases, which I did not know about until this week and wish I had. Every APRS message is transmitted in clear text and archived publicly and permanently, so addressing a phone by its raw number publishes that number, next to my callsign and a timestamp, on every single message. Go and look at the SMS gateway’s own traffic page on aprs.fi some time — it is a wall of other people’s phone numbers. The bridge accepts alias commands over the air, addressed to SMS:

#alias #add wife 2125550123        create it
@wife heading home, 20 minutes     use it, with no number on the air
#alias #remove wife 2125550123     delete it

The gateway answers “Alias Added.”, so you know it took.

The FT5D receiving the Alias Added confirmation back from the SMS gateway

Ten-digit numbers only for the alias commands, no leading 1; UK numbers take twelve. Be clear-eyed about what this buys: the number still crosses the air once, in the #alias #add message itself, and that packet is archived like all the others. It is one exposure instead of one per message. Sending the same command from the phone side instead keeps it off RF entirely, which is what I would do starting over.

With the alias in place the whole round trip runs without a phone number appearing anywhere on the air — I send @self ht to my phone! from the handheld, it arrives as an ordinary text, and the reply comes back to the radio addressed the same way.

A day later, with more data

Two numbers from a second look, one reassuring and one I cannot explain.

The whitelist, measured rather than asserted. Over thirty hours the APRS-IS servers offered this station 10,151 packets. The monitor fault, solved at last: one byte from a stranger

The web monitor had a fault I could not pin down for weeks. The page would freeze, or stop answering altogether, while the gateway carried on gating perfectly well. I built instrumentation for it, guessed at orphaned pipelines, tested that hypothesis, and recorded it as refuted. It took the monitor dying while I happened to be looking to get the answer, and the answer is small and slightly absurd.

Some station a few states away sends a DX bearing in its comment text:

DX: W8QT-1 71.9mi 286° 03:43

That degree sign is a Latin-1 byte, 0xB0, not UTF-8. Dire Wolf writes other people’s comments to the log exactly as they arrive, as it should. My monitor read that log through a pipeline whose output it decoded as strict UTF-8 — so the byte raised UnicodeDecodeError.

Here is the part that turned a cosmetic problem into a dead monitor. UnicodeDecodeError is a subclass of ValueError, and my read loop was wrapped in except (ValueError, OSError). So the exception was caught, the loop exited tidily, and a new pipeline was started — without killing the old one. Every one of those log lines said rc=None, which I had been reading as “the pipeline ended.” It means the opposite: the child was still running.

So each stray byte left a tail -f | gawk behind. Over days, dozens of them accumulated, until the Pi ran out of thread slots and the page started failing every request with RuntimeError("can't start new thread"). That was the symptom I kept chasing, and it was the last link in the chain rather than the first.

Three things hid this, and they are the transferable part.

I was throwing away the evidence on purpose. The pipeline was started with stderr redirected to /dev/null. The one output that would have named the cause was being discarded by design, in a program whose entire job is to show me what is happening.

systemd cleaned up the crime scene. When I finally went looking for orphaned pipelines with pgrep, I found none, and wrote the theory off. But restarting a unit kills its whole cgroup — and I had restarted it to get the page back before I went looking. The hypothesis was correct; the refutation was an artifact of checking after the cleanup.

A return code of None reads like an ending. It is the one value that means the process is still alive. I read past it in log lines I had written myself, for days.

The fix is two lines of intent. Decode with errors="replace", because it is not this program’s business to require that other operators’ comment text be well-formed — a stranger’s degree sign should cost me a replacement character, not a monitor. And when the reader stops, kill the pipeline’s process group rather than abandoning it, escalating to SIGKILL after five seconds, with a log line when it happens. The first change makes this particular fault impossible. The second makes any future variant self-limiting instead of cumulative.

I would have found it in an hour if the pipeline had been allowed to speak.

What this settles, and what it does not

Two of the three stages I laid out above are done: the reach is measured, and it is measured with the antenna fixed. The third still needs the car. Nothing here proves a message can reach me somewhere out of my own footprint — it proves there is a path that could carry one, and how often that path is available.

A UPS, a clock, and four layers against a pulled plug

The gateway runs unattended on a shelf, which means the question is not whether it will lose power but what happens when it does. That turned into a day of work with a clear ranking, and the useful part is the ranking rather than the parts.

The card is written far less than you would think, and that was already true. run/ is a tmpfs and the journal is in RAM, so the only continuous writer on the system is in memory. What was left were the files written rarely and carelessly: NetworkManager’s connection files, Direwolf’s own state, igate.local.conf.

Swap turned out to be writing to the card the whole time, invisibly. Raspberry Pi OS puts swap in compressed RAM — good — but its default mechanism is zram+file, which also keeps a /var/swap writeback file on the card and a timer that moves idle pages into it. swapon --show does not reveal this, because a writeback store is not a swap device. Mine was 444 MB and had been in use since the card was flashed. Mechanism=zram in /etc/rpi/swap.conf.d/ removes it and keeps the useful half.

ext4’s default journals metadata, not file contents. So a file being written when the power goes can be left torn even though the filesystem is consistent. data=journal journals data too, at a throughput cost that is meaningless on a machine whose steady-state card output is approximately nothing. It has to go on the kernel command line rather than in fstab — data= cannot be changed by a remount — which is the kind of detail that turns a one-line change into an afternoon.

Then the UPS HAT, which is where the reasoning gets interesting.

The UPS HAT seated on the GPIO header, with its cell and the ferrite on the radio’s USB lead

It fits because the 40-pin header was free. This gateway’s only GPIO use is CM108_GPIO, which is a pin inside the USB sound card rather than on the Pi — so the three pins the HAT needs were never contended for. The radio interface runs off USB and the power board runs off the header, and neither knows the other exists.

The cell is marked 750 mAh, which is worth mentioning only because the product listing said 450 and I had repeated that figure through four documents before photographing the thing. The runtime it implies I have still not measured, and nothing here depends on it: the shutdown takes thirty seconds.

The battery is not the protection. The shutdown is.

A UPS that keeps the Pi running and then dies mid-write corrupts the card exactly as a pulled plug does — it just moves the moment. What closes the requirement is detecting the loss and powering off with charge still in hand. The HAT reports on GPIO: pin 17 goes high on mains loss, pin 27 carries a heartbeat, and pin 18 is held low to mean “the Pi is running” — the kernel releasing it at power-off is what tells the HAT to cut its own output and come back when mains returns.

I did not use the vendor’s script as shipped. It is a SysV init script, so nothing restarts it if the loop dies; it logs nothing, so a shutdown at 3 a.m. leaves no explanation; its pin exports are unguarded, so after any restart it polls pins it never configured; and its timer is 60 seconds with documentation claiming 5, which would take an unattended station off the air for a mains flicker. Mine waits 30 seconds, logs every transition to the journal, and runs under systemd with Restart=always.

One design choice in it looks wrong and is deliberate: it uses the deprecated sysfs GPIO interface rather than the modern libgpiod. A libgpiod line is released when the holding process exits — which would drop pin 18 and have the HAT cut power to a perfectly healthy Pi every time the service was stopped. An exported sysfs pin keeps its value after the exporting process dies. The obsolete interface has the safety property the modern one lacks, for this particular job.

And the guard was wrong on the first attempt. An unconnected GPIO17 is a floating pin whose value means nothing, so the script must not act on it without the heartbeat. My first version treated the HAT as present until proven absent, with a grace period — and tested against a simulated GPIO tree with no heartbeat at all, it powered the machine off anyway, because the countdown beat the grace period. It now treats the HAT as absent until a toggle proves otherwise, which makes the guard independent of the timer. I would not have found that by installing it and hoping.

The clock was the quiet second benefit. The HAT carries a DS3231, and until now this station came up with the wrong time and timestamped every packet it gated until NTP caught up. The cyberdeck had already paid the whole learning cost — rtc-ds1307 is the right driver for a DS3231 despite the name, hwclock lives in util-linux-extra rather than util-linux, and /dev/i2c-1 needs the i2c-dev module that neither the overlay nor dtparam loads. All three of those were written down in my design document and none of them were in my build script, which is the gap between recording a lesson and automating it.

What is not done is the test. Pulling the plug five or six times and confirming each boot comes back clean. Simulated GPIO is not a power cut, and none of this is finished until that has been done.

The antenna finally went up — 2026-10-03

Three positions in three weeks, and the article above has them in order: a dual-band mobile whip on a camera tripod in the living room, which turned out to be causing the resets; then the N9TAX slim jim dangling from the porch roof, which fixed them; and now the same slim jim up a telescoping mast standing about fifteen feet above the lawn, with a guy line off one side, well away from the house and its wiring.

The porch was always temporary. What it bought was a clean answer to the RF question — the common-mode current was gone the moment the antenna stopped using the feedline as its missing half. What it did not buy was height, or any real distance from the building. The mast buys both.

The N9TAX slim jim on a telescoping mast in the yard, roughly fifteen feet up, clear of the house

The station moved with it. The radio, its bench supply and the pi-gate are off the dining table and onto a small folding side table in the corner of a spare room.

The FT-2900R, its supply and the pi-gate in the corner of a spare room — the whole station

The feedline was the bigger problem — 2026-10-04

Standing wave ratio at the radio, through the full 50-foot run, measured 1.2:1. That looks like a finished job and is almost meaningless, because the run was RG316.

RG316 is thin PTFE coax, electrically close to RG174, and at 146 MHz it loses roughly 9 to 10 dB per hundred feet. Fifty feet is therefore about 5 dB each way. Line loss masks SWR, and at 5 dB it masks it almost completely: the reflection comes back 10 dB down no matter what the antenna is doing, so a completely disconnected cable would still have read about 1.9:1. A reading of 1.2 was consistent with an excellent antenna and with a mediocre one, and it could not tell them apart.

The 5 dB was the real finding. At 30 watts out of the radio, something like 9 or 10 watts was reaching the antenna, and every signal received arrived 5 dB weaker than it left the feedpoint.

Fifty feet of RG-8X loses about 1.8 dB instead — 3.5 dB recovered in both directions, which is more than doubling effective radiated power while also improving receive, something extra transmit power can never do. It went in on 10-04.

It is worth putting this beside the earlier result. Eight decibels more power bought nothing measurable, because the antenna was the limit. Once the antenna stopped being the limit, the feedline became it — and the feedline was the cheapest thing on the station to fix.

One consequence to expect rather than be alarmed by: SWR read at the radio goes up on the better cable. With only 1.8 dB of loss, a disconnected cable reads about 5:1 instead of 1.9:1, so the meter can finally see the antenna at all. A number that climbs from 1.2 to 1.5 is the masking lifting, not a fault appearing.

What has not been measured is whether any of this changed the numbers. reach has been run twice, both times from the porch and on RG316: 42 of 112 beacons repeated on 09-27, then 41 of 68 on 09-28 — 37 percent against 60 percent, a difference that is statistically real and was never explained.

It is now unexplainable, and that is worth being plain about rather than quietly dropping. Antenna height, station location and feedline have all changed since those runs. Nothing measured from here is comparable with them, so the 37-versus-60 question is closed unanswered. What replaces it is two fresh reach runs on different days from the finished station, as a new baseline — not a comparison with the old pair, and not a conclusion drawn from whichever number arrives first.

Checking deviation without a deviation meter — 2026-10-03

With the antenna on the mast, the next question was whether the transmitter was putting a correctly shaped signal into it. The target for 1200-baud AFSK on a 5 kHz wide-FM channel is 2.5 to 3.0 kHz peak deviation. Under that, the tones arrive weak at a distant receiver’s discriminator.

The upper bound deserves care, because it is easy to overstate and I did overstate it at first. Nothing falls outside a receiver’s IF filter at 3 kHz. Carson puts 2.8 kHz deviation at 10.0 kHz occupied, 4 kHz at 12.4 and 5 kHz at 14.4, against a 2 m FM receiver’s IF commonly around 15 kHz wide — so 4 kHz is still comfortably inside it. What actually goes wrong above the target is the radio’s voice processing starts acting on the data. Pre-emphasis, +6 dB per octave on a microphone input, already has the 2200 Hz tone deviating more than the 1200 Hz one. The deviation limiter is a hard clipper capping peaks near 5 kHz. Behind it is the splatter filter, whose job is removing the harmonics the clipping just made. Some radios add a microphone compressor on top.

All of that is designed around speech, which tolerates clipping without sounding wrong. AFSK is two tones whose relative deviation has to survive intact, and it does not — the margin the demodulator uses to tell mark from space narrows, bit errors rise, and one bad bit fails the CRC and discards the entire frame. There is no partial credit.

Adjacent-channel energy rises with deviation too, and the whole network is aligned to the same convention, so matching it is what makes one setting work into everybody’s receiver.

The direct instrument is a deviation meter, which I do not own. The indirect one is any receiver with a band scope, through Carson’s rule:

BW = 2 (dF + Fmax)        rearranged:   dF = BW/2 - Fmax

dF is the peak deviation. Fmax is the highest modulating audio frequency, which for APRS is 2200 Hz — the space tone.

The FTX-1’s band scope on 144.390 at a 20 kHz span. Blue is the pi-gate transmitting from the yard; the wide orange trace is a handheld acknowledging from inside the same room

The blue trace occupies about 10 kHz, which gives

dF = 10/2 - 2.2 = 2.8 kHz

Inside the target band at the level that was already configured, so the loop ran zero times. That is where my level happened to sit and not a reason to skip the measurement — the same interface on a different radio, or the same radio on a different input, lands somewhere else. The step that matters is step four: change TX_AUDIO_LEVEL, transmit again, read it again, until the number is in the window.

Two things are worth saying about the method rather than the number.

The reading is tighter than it sounds. Against gridlines at 4 and 8 kHz either side of centre, the edges of that blue trace are good to about 100 Hz, which puts the deviation at 2.8 kHz give or take 50. The looser number is in Carson’s rule itself: it describes the bandwidth holding about 98 percent of the power, so the remaining question is which contour on the display corresponds to that, and a scope with more dynamic range shows sidebands below it. That is a systematic offset rather than a reading error, and at 2.8 kHz there is enough room on both sides of the target that it does not change the answer.

The wide orange trace is not a measurement of anything. That is the handheld acknowledging from a few feet away, with the FTX-1’s antenna disconnected, and the width is front-end compression in the receiver rather than the handheld’s deviation. A signal strong enough to overload the front end reads meaningless bandwidth. Only a signal arriving at a normal level can be measured this way — which is exactly why the pi-gate had to be out in the yard for the blue trace to mean something.

If the number had come out wrong, the adjustment is TX_AUDIO_LEVEL in the radio profile, and Dire Wolf generates the test tone itself: -x m for a steady 1200 Hz mark, -x s for the 2200 Hz space, -x a alternating, -x p to key PTT in silence. The gateway service has to be stopped first, because the sound card takes one user at a time, and the result is an unmodulated carrier — a dummy load or an unused simplex frequency, never 144.390.

There is a second method that needs only a spectrum display rather than a calibrated one. An FM carrier’s centre component vanishes at a modulation index of 2.405, and the index is deviation divided by tone frequency, so a 1200 Hz mark tone nulls its own carrier at exactly 2.89 kHz deviation. Raise the audio level until the carrier collapses into the noise and the setting is calibrated against a mathematical constant rather than a meter. The space tone cannot be used for it: its first null would require 5.29 kHz, past the channel limit.

One detail specific to this station: the Digirig feeds the radio’s microphone input, which normally carries pre-emphasis and a limiter. Pre-emphasis boosts the 2200 Hz tone relative to the 1200 Hz one, so the space tone reaches the limiter first — and once the limiter is engaged, more audio level buys distortion instead of deviation.

I finally pulled the plug, and almost everything was wrong — 2026-10-04

The UPS section above ends by saying the test had not been done: pulling the plug and confirming the station goes down cleanly. Two days later I did it. The shutdown worked, to the second. Getting there turned up three separate faults in software I had already written up as finished, and then one in the hardware that matters more than any of them.

It had never run, not once

The first thing I checked was the service’s own log, and it was not there at all. Not a startup line, nothing. What was there was this, repeating:

ups: GPIO17 did not appear under /sys/class/gpio — is the kernel built with GPIO_SYSFS?
igate-ups.service: Scheduled restart job, restart counter is at 11734.

Eleven thousand seven hundred and thirty-four restarts, every five seconds since the last boot, and the message blames the wrong thing. /sys/class/gpio existed. What it contained was gpiochip512 and gpiochip566, not gpiochip0.

The sysfs GPIO numbering base moved. It used to be that the Pi’s main controller sat at base 0 and BCM 17 was /sys/class/gpio/gpio17; since kernel 6.6 gpiolib allocates dynamically, and on this Pi the base is 512. So the real pins are 529, 530 and 539. My script hardcoded the BCM numbers, the export was silently refused, and the error message I had written for a missing kernel feature fired on an off-by-512.

That is bad enough on its own. What makes it worse is the consequence. GPIO18 held low is what tells the HAT “the Pi is alive,” and the service had never held it. So for two days the HAT had seen no reason to believe anything was running, and every mains loss was an abrupt cut — the UPS was a liability rather than protection, and the clean reboot I thought I had seen on 10-03 was nothing of the kind.

The service now reads the base from the controller rather than assuming it.

Even fixed, it could not have fired

With the pins right it armed. Then it printed this, two seconds apart:

ups: no UPS heartbeat on GPIO27 — not acting on GPIO17 until it returns
ups: UPS heartbeat is back

The HAT toggles GPIO27 about every half second, and the script tested for it by reading the pin twice a tenth of a second apart. A tenth of a second against a half-second half-period catches an edge about one time in five. I measured it against a simulated HAT: 19 misses out of 25.

Three consecutive misses made the script declare the HAT absent, and declaring the HAT absent zeroes the countdown. So even with the pin numbers corrected, a real mains loss would almost certainly never have survived sixty consecutive seconds of counting. The service would have armed, logged, looked healthy in every way I would have thought to check, and been structurally incapable of doing its job.

The window has to be longer than one half-period for a negative result to mean anything. Five reads across 0.8 seconds: 0 misses in 30, and still correctly reports a dead pin.

And the guard against the storm was inert

I had added a restart limit after seeing the 11,735 figure. systemd said:

Unknown key 'StartLimitIntervalSec' in section [Service], ignoring.

They are [Unit] keys. In [Service] they load without complaint beyond that one line and do nothing. Which is how a crash loop filled the 32 MB persistent journal and evicted the history of the reboots the journal had been turned on to explain — I lost the record of one outage to the mechanism that was supposed to describe it.

A watchdog should not cost 15% of the machine

Fixing the heartbeat made it worse before it made it better. systemctl status reported 8 minutes 35 seconds of CPU in 56 minutes — fifteen percent of a core, continuously, on a Pi whose actual job is running a software modem.

All of it was forking. Each pin read was a subshell plus a cat; each wait was a fork of /bin/sleep; the clock was a fork of /bin/date. Several times a second, forever. Replacing the reads with bash’s read builtin, the clock with EPOCHSECONDS, and the waits with read -t on a fifo that never delivers anything took it from 15% to 5.9% to 2.6% to 0.6%. There are no forks left in the loop.

Then the test

Two monitors, one on the Pi and one meant to be on the laptop, and the plug out for three minutes.

13:10:01  ups: mains lost — on battery, powering off in 60s unless it returns
13:10:11  ups: still on battery (10s of 60s)
...
13:10:51  ups: still on battery (50s of 60s)
13:11:01  ups: powering off after 60s on battery

Sixty seconds to the second. The boot’s journal ends at 13:11:04, so the shutdown itself took three. And on the way back up there is no recovering journal in dmesg — the first time this station has gone down without a dirty unmount.

The cancel path had been checked separately an hour earlier, by restoring mains at fourteen seconds: mains restored after 14s — shutdown cancelled, and the station stayed up. Both branches work.

The part I did not expect

Then I plugged it back in, and nothing happened.

No ping. No hostname. A red power LED on the Pi and a blinking green one on the HAT, but the Pi’s own green activity LED was dark. The momentary button did nothing. What eventually cleared it was disconnecting the cell and power-cycling.

My first explanation was that the HAT had cut its output and refused to bring it back. That was wrong, and the mistake is instructive: the mains adapter plugs into the Pi, not the HAT. The vendor’s guide says so plainly — primary power goes to the Pi’s micro-USB jack and reaches the HAT through the GPIO header. The HAT never has to restore anything. The Pi powers itself the moment mains is back.

Provided it was genuinely off. And it was not.

A Raspberry Pi starts when its 5 volt rail collapses. poweroff halts the processor and leaves the rail up — that is why the red LED stays lit, and it is normal. So the Pi was sitting halted, still drawing from the cell, waiting for a power cut that was never going to come. Because the instant I plugged mains back in, the HAT started charging. The cell would never flatten. The rail would never collapse. It would have sat there until I disconnected the battery, which is exactly what I ended up doing, three times in one afternoon.

A clean shutdown had turned a three-minute outage into one that lasted until I walked into the room.

Four ways to tell the HAT to let go, none of which worked

The HAT does have a disconnect. Its script says driving GPIO18 high starts a ten-second timer to cut the output. I tested that directly: on battery, with everything else stopped, I drove the pin high and left the Pi running. The station went dark within the minute. The mechanism is real.

Combining it with a shutdown is where it falls apart.

Letting the pin float. The vendor’s own script relies on the kernel releasing GPIO18 at power-off and the resulting Hi-Z being read as high. It is not, at least not here. Nothing happened.

Driving it high and leaving a pull-up. My first fix wrote a 1 to the pin and then set it to an input with the internal pull-up, so the level would survive the kernel letting go. Around fifty kilohms is too weak for the HAT to read as a high. I had driven the pin hard for about a tenth of a second and then quietly let it go floppy. This is the test that produced the four-minute lit LED, and I spent an hour believing the board was at fault on the strength of it.

Driving it high as a real output, then halting. Twice — once from the monitor loop immediately before poweroff, and once from a hook in /usr/lib/systemd/system-shutdown/, which systemd runs after every filesystem is unmounted and the root remounted read-only. That hook is the correct place for this; it is the last thing that runs. Neither worked. The halt releases the pad, GPIO18 falls back to its default pull-down, and the ten-second timer is cancelled before it expires.

The vendor’s own script, unmodified. By this point the obvious suspect was my own choice of interface. I had used sysfs deliberately, because an exported pin keeps its value after the exporting process dies, where libgpiod releases a line the moment its holder exits — and that release is a hazard if the service ever stops. The vendor’s script uses libgpiod. Which means systemd would release the line early in shutdown, while the Pi was still running, giving the HAT’s timer time to expire. That is a good theory and it explains everything.

So I fetched ups-gpiod.sh from their repository, stopped mine, removed its sysfs exports, ran theirs as root, and pulled the plug. It counted down, printed ### Powering off..., and shut the Pi down cleanly. Then the red LED stayed lit and the HAT’s green LED kept flashing fast — discharging into a machine that was no longer running. Identical to every other attempt.

The pin has to stay high for the HAT to act. Only a running Pi can hold it high. A running Pi is precisely what a shutdown cannot leave behind.

There is no sequence that works, and it is not my implementation. The one test that could have blamed me cleared me.

I also considered the momentary button, since the guide describes it as toggling the HAT on and off — some latched state from a press days earlier. The HAT had been fully de-powered between attempts several times, mains off and cell disconnected, which leaves its microcontroller with no supply at all. Then I ran their script a fifth time after deliberately pressing the button to toggle it. Identical failure.

The button did turn out to be good for something, though. Press it while the Pi is stranded halted-on-battery and the output cuts immediately; plug mains back in and it boots. That is a far better recovery than disconnecting the cell, which is how I did the first several. It is also the one part of the documentation that behaved exactly as written.

One detail made all of this harder to reason about than it should have been: on a BCM2837 the pull-up/pull-down registers are write-only. pinctrl get 18 shows the direction and the level but prints -- for the pull, because the hardware cannot report it. Every hypothesis about pull resistors had to be tested by watching an LED in another room.

What the HAT is actually for

It is a good board for a different problem. It will carry a Pi for half an hour without a hiccup, which is what you want for a logger or a camera or anything that keeps doing useful work while the lights are out. It will run a Pi deliberately on battery. And its product page promises exactly what it delivers: shut down safely when the battery runs low, and restart when power returns. That is coherent — if the shutdown fires near depletion, the cell flattens a few minutes later, the rail collapses, and mains return boots it.

The trap is a short timer with a full cell. The vendor’s script ships with sixty seconds, so I doubt I am the first person to fall into it.

My case is the one it cannot serve: unattended, must come back by itself, and gains nothing from staying up — because the radio runs from its own mains supply and is dark from the first second of any outage regardless. Keeping the Pi alive through a power cut buys a gateway with nothing to gate.

Where it leaves the station

The HAT comes off. An Adafruit PiRTC goes on instead — the same DS3231, at the same I²C address, so the configuration does not change at all. It takes a CR1220, which is not included; non-rechargeable is the right choice, because that board has no charging circuit and the chip draws microamps, so one cell lasts five years. I passed over the three-dollar DS3231 For Pi modules because several of them carry a charge path meant for a rechargeable cell, and a slowly-cooked lithium coin in a sealed enclosure is not a saving. The clock was the only part of the HAT this station ever needed.

Until it arrives, the HAT stays on the header with its cell unplugged and the service disabled, which amounts to the same thing: a DS3231 and nothing else.

What that gives me:

  • Mains lost — the Pi cuts instantly. No graceful shutdown.
  • Mains restored — it boots by itself, every time, with nobody present.
  • The clock — right before the network is even associated, so not one mistimestamped packet.

The card is protected by the four layers that were always doing the real work and have nothing to do with any of this: run/ on tmpfs, the journal in RAM and capped, zram with no writeback file, and the root mounted data=journal. A hard cut on a system that writes almost nothing is a modest risk, and I have traded it for never being stranded.

So I end the week where I started it, minus thirty-five dollars and plus a lithium cell I no longer have sitting in an unattended enclosure. The requirement was always met by the filesystem work; the HAT was an attempt to improve on it that made things worse.

What I would take from this

The three software faults have one thing in common, and it is not that GPIO is fiddly. The original test built its own /sys/class/gpio tree and then checked the script against it. It could only ever confirm the arithmetic it was already using. It passed for two days while the real service crash-looped eleven thousand times, and three documents — this article among them — described the feature as fitted and verified.

A test that constructs its own fixture tests the fixture.

The hardware lesson is narrower and I would not have guessed it: a graceful shutdown is worth nothing on an unattended machine unless something will turn it back on. I had been treating “shuts down cleanly” as the finish line. It is half a requirement. The other half is the part nobody writes on the box.
