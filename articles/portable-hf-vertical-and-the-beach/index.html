Converting the Rigid V Dipole to a Quarter-Wave Vertical, and Taking It to the Beach

Published on: August 12, 2026  · 

The rigid V dipole has been my most-used portable antenna since I built it. It goes up in five minutes, it needs no tuner on 20 m, and it fits in a bag. The one thing it could not do was give me a low-angle vertical pattern, which is exactly what I wanted for a trip to the shore in August.

It turned out I did not need to build anything new. The hub already had every part required — I just had not noticed what I was looking at.

The result: a 20 m quarter wave standing on dry sand, well back from the surf

The Realization

My hub is a 3D-printed hexagon with threaded studs on its faces, riding on a camera tripod’s ball head. In dipole configuration, two 18 ft telescoping whips screw into two adjacent faces at a 120° included angle, and each whip is extended to roughly 5 m.

Five meters is the part that matters. A quarter wave on 20 m is 5.03 m. Each leg of the V dipole was already a 20 m quarter-wave radiator — I had two of them, and I was using them against each other.

A vertical is what you get when you stop using the second leg as a radiator and start using it as a ground. So the conversion is not a rebuild. It is a change of mind about what the two halves are for:

  1. Tilt the ball head, and with it the hex hub, until the driven side points straight up. One whip, vertical, is now the radiator.
  2. Bolt a bundle of ground radials to the stud on the other side — the face that used to hold the second whip. That side is the return path.
  3. Leave the 1:1 common mode choke exactly where it was.

The hub tilted over: whip on top, choke at the left, radial bundle bolted to the stud on the right

That is the whole modification. No new hardware, no drilling, nothing cut. The same hub does both jobs, and switching between them is a matter of loosening one ball head and moving one bundle of wire.

The Radials

The ground plane is the part that actually took work, and it is the part worth copying.

I ended up with 20 radials, 10 ft each, from 22 AWG silicone-insulated hookup wire, crimped into ring terminals in bundles of five — four lugs, five wires apiece, all four stacked on the one stud.

It started as ten. The driveway photographs below are that first pair of lugs, before I made the second ten and doubled the field.

The first two lugs, five wires crimped into each, stacked on the stud under a nut

Three properties of that wire earned their place:

  • Silicone insulation lays dead flat. It has no coil memory. Wire that remembers being on a spool spends the whole session trying to curl back up, and on sand it will crawl into a bird’s nest while you are looking at the radio.
  • It came cut to length. Twenty identical radials without twenty measurements and forty crimps of my own.
  • It came in assorted colors. This sounds decorative and is not. When you are fanning twenty wires out on a beach and then gathering them again in the dark, being able to say “follow the yellow one” is the difference between a five-minute teardown and a knot.

Ten feet is about 0.145 λ on 20 m, well short of a resonant quarter wave, and that is deliberate. Radials lying on the ground are not resonant elements — they are a screen whose job is to intercept return currents before the lossy earth does. For that job, the literature has been consistent since the 1930s: many short radials beat a few long ones. Twenty short wires from one bag buys more than four carefully cut ones.

The first ten laid out on the driveway. The other ten came later, and doubled this

20 m First, and It Worked

The first thing I did with the vertical was the simplest possible thing: whip at about 5 m, no coil, no matching network, straight onto 20 m. It worked immediately — a good match on the first sweep, and QSOs in the log before I had tried anything else. Measured on the driveway on the evening of August 3:

14.225 MHz, 1.15:1, 56.7 − 3.72j. No coil, no tuner, no matching network

That reading is the whole case for the conversion. A quarter wave over a ground plane, fed directly, with nothing between the coax and the antenna but the choke that was already there.

It does one band at a time, which is not the same thing as being a single-band antenna. Swept 1–30 MHz at the 20 m setting, the rest of the spectrum sits exactly where a 5 m radiator leaves it:

But the whip telescopes. I adjust its length for 17 m and 15 m, and those two bands along with 20 m are what I actually travel with — the right bands for a small station a long way from home.

Adjusting the whip is a deliberate choice over the alternatives. I am not trying to find one compromise length that half-matches three bands, and I am not interested in eating a high SWR or burning the difference in a tuner. Given the choice, I will always pull the antenna back down the mast or the tripod, reset the whips, and redeploy. At 5 or 10 watts there is nothing to spare for a matching network to waste. A tuner turns a mismatch into a comfortable-looking meter reading; it does not put the power back in the air. Two minutes of telescoping does.

Packing It

The entire station, antenna included, fits in a padded camera bag — which is the correct container for this antenna, since the tripod and ball head are camera parts anyway.

Radio, choke, hub, whips, tripod and the radial bundle, in a single camera bag

The radial bundle is the only awkward item, and the color coding is what keeps it from becoming the item that ruins the bag.

The Beach

August 12, dry sand, well back from the waterline, about an hour before sunset. Setup took about as long as it takes to describe: tripod down, whip up, twenty radials walked out in a circle, coax on.

Twenty radials on sand. They lay where you put them, which is the entire argument for silicone insulation

Then the measurement I had come for:

The wideband sweep on the beach, for comparison against the driveway numbers

Nothing moved. A concrete driveway in a valley and a sand beach beside the Atlantic put the antenna within 1.3 Ω and 3.5 Ω of each other — a difference smaller than what I get by collapsing and re-extending the whip.

It is worth being honest that this is not a clean two-point comparison. The ground was not the only thing that changed between those two sweeps: the driveway measurement was taken on the first ten radials and the beach measurement on all twenty. Two variables moved, and the feedpoint still did not. That makes the result less useful as a statement about ground type specifically, and arguably more striking as a statement about how little any of it reaches the feedpoint.

There is a wrinkle in that second variable worth spelling out, because it runs opposite to intuition. A quarter wave over a perfect ground plane has a radiation resistance near 36 Ω, not 50 Ω. Every ohm of ground loss adds to it, and the analyzer reads the sum — so a worse radial field reads closer to 50 Ω and returns a prettier SWR. Improving the ground system moves the feedpoint away from 1:1. It is not unusual for SWR to climb a little as the radial field gets better, and a ground-mounted vertical showing a flawless match is often telling you how much of the power the dirt is absorbing.

The resistance here did fall, 56.7 Ω to 55.4 Ω, which is the direction fewer losses would push it. I would not read much into 1.3 Ω, and most of the tick from 1.15 to 1.19 is the reactance rather than the resistance. But it is the right sign, and it is worth knowing which way that arrow points before treating a low SWR as a grade.

This is the part worth being careful about, because it is easy to tell the wrong story in either direction.

The wrong story in one direction is that the beach did nothing. The wrong story in the other is that a flat SWR proves the antenna is working well. Neither follows, because the feedpoint impedance is not where the beach was ever going to show up.

A quarter wave with twenty on-ground radials has a near field dominated by those radials — that is what they are for, and it is why the match travels so well. The thing salt water does is happen in the far field: the reflection that forms the low-angle lobe takes place hundreds of feet out, over ground with a conductivity perhaps two orders of magnitude better than a Pennsylvania hillside. A NanoVNA at the base has no way to see that. It is looking at the wrong end of the problem.

So the measurement says one useful thing and refuses to say another. It says the antenna is repeatable — it lands on the same match over wildly different ground, which for a portable antenna is exactly the property you want and is not guaranteed. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the signal got out better, and I did not run the controlled comparison that would answer that. I have no A/B, no reference antenna, and no log I would stake a claim on. Anyone who tells you their SWR improved at the beach is telling you about their ground losses, not their DX.

What I Take From This

The conversion is the good part, and it cost nothing. A hub built for a 120° V dipole is already a vertical mount, because a dipole leg cut for a band is already a quarter wave for that band, and the only question is what you connect to the other half. Anyone with a hex hub and a tripod ball head can do this today with parts they already own, plus a bag of wire.

The radial wire is the piece I would not change. Twenty pre-cut, dead-flat, color-coded 10 ft radials turned the ground plane from the reason not to bring the vertical into a thirty-second job, and 20, 17 and 15 m are all covered by resetting the whip.

The honest scoreboard on the beach itself is short. The match is repeatable across ground types, which is worth knowing. Whether the salt water bought me the low-angle lobe I went there for is a question I did not actually answer, and answering it needs a reference antenna and a pile of spots rather than a VNA and a nice view. That is the next experiment, and it will probably look like the WSPR comparisons rather than like this article.
