A Lightweight Mobile Setup with the Yaesu FT5DR

Published on: August 01, 2026  · 

The FT5DR and speaker mic riding on the Ram seat-gap wedge mount, passenger side

I’ve been wanting a second name-brand HT to go alongside my Yaesu VX6R for a while. I’ve got an inexpensive Radtel 880G that gets used plenty, but I don’t fully trust it the way I trust an established name brand, especially for anything more than casual listening.

Why APRS, and Why the FT5DR

The bigger draw was APRS. I’d played around with beaconing and messaging on my FTX1 Optima and had a lot of fun with it. I used an SMS gateway service that let me send an APRS message from the radio, it got picked up by a local W3YA digipeater on the APRS frequency (144.390 MHz), relayed through someone’s igate to the internet, and from there to the SMS service and onto my cell phone. Getting a text message to arrive after bouncing through a chain of amateur infrastructure like that was genuinely cool, and I wanted a way to do something similar when I’m away from my QTH.

I’d also been spending time on aprs.fi watching who else I knew was active and thinking about who I could try to message. Eventually I’d like to set up my own igate with strictly whitelisted two-way traffic from the internet, so I can APRS message my wife’s phone and she can message me back if I’m in range, but that’s a project for another day.

The FT5DR stood out as a good second name-brand HT that also does APRS well. It being on sale for \$100 off ended up being the deciding factor.

Setup was easy. I worked out how to change settings, program memories, and get APRS running entirely without touching the manual, since the UI was intuitive enough to figure out by feel. I read the manual afterward, mostly just to make sure I hadn’t missed any of the finer points.

First Outing: Front Porch Net

One of the first things I did with the radio was set it up on my front porch with the speaker mic and a N6TAX slim jim, strung up a 33 ft telescoping mast (about two-thirds extended), and checked into my local club’s Sunday net.

APRS on the Move

Once I had APRS dialed in, beaconing my position and checking aprs.fi as I moved around became a regular habit. I also like getting occasional weather beacons and seeing what my APRS path was on APRS.fi.

Going Mobile, Without a Mobile Rig

Next I started looking at ways to turn this into a genuinely portable mobile setup. I’m not interested in a dedicated mobile rig; an HT paired with a magmount quarter-wave NMO antenna (a Signal Stick, from Signal Stuff) and a plug-in power source is plenty appealing on its own.

Attempt One: Cupholder Mount

The first thing I tried, for a few weeks, was a cupholder-style Ram mount, a piece that expands to hold itself tightly inside a cupholder, with a standard 1 in Ram ball-and-arm system on top. I didn’t bother with Ram-branded components; inexpensive equivalents from Amazon worked fine.

The cupholder-style Ram mount

At first I just clipped the radio on and used it to listen to local activity and send APRS beacons. Later I picked up a different attachment that let the radio and speaker mic ride side by side.

The FT5DR clipped into the cupholder mount

As a bonus, this same mount turned out to work really well for my cell phone, since it’s in an Otterbox Defender case with a belt-clip attachment.

After about a week of driving around with it, though, I missed having my second cupholder back, and I started to worry about the lever moment the mount put on the cupholder itself. The plastic on my car’s cupholder seems pretty thin, and I didn’t want it to fatigue or crack over time from the vibration.

Attempt Two: Ram Seat-Gap Wedge Mount

The better solution turned out to be a Ram-branded seat-gap wedge mount, a hard plastic wedge inside a neoprene sleeve, designed to sit between the driver or passenger seat and the center console. It has a Ram ball mount on the top corner for attaching arms and device holders, plus an inflatable bladder inside the sleeve that you pump up with a blood-pressure-cuff-style bulb, the same basic idea as the plastic wedges you’d find at a hardware store for prying and shimming doors and windows.

This setup is rock solid, puts the radio in a much better spot than the cupholder ever did, and leaves both cupholders free for actual cups. It also comes out and moves to a different car in about two minutes flat, which I really like. It’s pictured above, riding in my wife’s car while I was in the passenger seat.

At around \$100, it was more than I wanted to spend, and I looked seriously at building my own version first. Those plans crept toward \$50 or more in materials and were speculative at best, with no guarantee it would actually hold up. Once I had the Ram mount in hand and saw how well it’s designed and built, I was satisfied it earned the price.

Final Thoughts

I’m looking forward to running this setup over the next several months with both the VX6R and the FT5DR. The VX6R is a fun radio to have along for travel since it can scan/receive CB and GMRS in addition to scan/receive/transmit on 2m and 70cm. The FT5DR earns its spot by letting me park one VFO on local repeaters as I drive through an area while simultaneously beaconing APRS or monitoring 146.520 for calls.
