An APRS Downlink Measured Around State College

Published on: November 07, 2026  · 

My APRS uplink worked from everywhere I tried. My downlink reached the radio four times out of ten.

Direction Messages Reached the radio Acknowledged
Handheld to phone 4 4 4
Phone to handheld 10 4 2

The cause is frame length at the one digipeater my handheld reliably reaches. W3YA-1 repeats my 75-byte beacons and none of my 120-byte gated messages, so the mountaintop that carries my uplink cannot carry my downlink. The limit is in my signal at that digipeater rather than in its configuration, which repeats other stations’ frames up to 97 bytes.

On the morning of November 7 I drove around State College sending APRS messages in both directions between a Yaesu FT5D handheld (KD3CCO-7) and my phone, with my own iGate (KD3CCO-10) as the bridge. The phone side goes through the APRS SMS gateway, so a text becomes an APRS message addressed to the handheld and the replies come back as texts.

Every frame is in my logs. Whether a message arrived is a separate question the logs cannot answer, and I got that by reading the message list on the radio afterward. My first pass skipped that step and undercounted the downlink by half.

The handheld ran its stock rubber duck for every message. No mag mount, no external antenna. The South Atherton sites were from inside a moving vehicle. The Fraser Street garage was from inside the vehicle too, parked, on the fifth floor. The two Elixer Cafe sites were on foot, one inside the building and one on the sidewalk outside.

A rubber duck inside a car body is a poor receive antenna sitting in the car’s electrical noise. My iGate has an antenna 28 ft up feeding a dedicated receiver. The radio path itself is reciprocal, so the difference sits at the receiving ends, and the handheld’s end is the worse of the two.

Being in a moving car also means a message sent at one corner could be received at the next. The gateway retries over several minutes and the car did not wait.

How the logs answer this

Two files do two jobs. The dated CSV log carries a utime per frame, so it answers when. It has a heard column holding the station a frame was copied from, which identifies a direct copy but not the whole path. Direwolf’s text log carries the full path with the used-entry marker, so it answers how, but it has no timestamps. Matching the two by frame count and ordering gives both.

Neither file knows whether a message arrived. The SMS gateway resends up to three times until an ack comes back, which is tempting to read as a delivery signal. It measures the round trip instead. Two messages in this set reached the handheld and were retried the full three times anyway, because no ack came back.

So the retry count sets a lower bound on failure. Delivery came from reading the radio’s own message list, which held four of the ten. My first pass at this analysis undercounted the downlink by half because I skipped that step.

Luna de Luna, Nittany Avenue and Alpha Fire Company are all on South Atherton and all three arrived. Westerly Parkway, also on South Atherton, did not. Six consecutive failures came from one address downtown, four from inside the building and two from the sidewalk. The one sent from five floors up in the same neighborhood arrived and was acknowledged in 7 seconds. The cafe and the garage are a few blocks apart, so distance does not separate those groups.

A rubber duck inside a car is the worst receive setup of the morning, and that is the setup that worked, three times out of four. The six failures came with the radio out of the car, two of them from a downtown sidewalk with no vehicle shell and no building in the way. Taking the handheld out of the car improved its antenna situation and the downlink stopped working.

Mobility does not explain it either. The appealing story for the corridor result is that a moving car samples new ground between retries while a stationary operator gets three attempts at the same spot. The Fraser garage message does not fit that: the car was parked, the message went out once, and the ack came back in 7 seconds.

One weakness stands. The South Atherton messages were sent to a moving car, so the three that arrived could have been received anywhere along that stretch rather than at the named corner. The corridor result is about the corridor, not about four intersections.

Two acknowledgements that never came back

Luna and Nittany reached the radio and the gateway kept resending them, so no ack completed the trip. Neither ack2915 nor ack2919 appears in my logs in any form, and the gateway’s continued retries suggest no other gateway saw them.

I do not know why. The handheld’s own messages got out from harder places that same morning, including a direct copy from inside the cafe, so a blanket uplink failure does not fit. The radio may have received a later retry somewhere else along the route and not acknowledged it, or the acks may have been transmitted and lost by everyone. Nothing in these logs separates those.

The practical result is that the gateway and I disagreed about four messages out of ten. Two it treated as failed had arrived, and I had no way to know.

Elevation

Terrain was the next thing I checked. Downtown State College sits in a valley, my station is uphill from it, and the digipeater that matters is on a mountain. Ground elevations from USGS 10 m data:

Ground elevation does not correlate with success. The two sites that failed are Elixer at 1149 ft and Westerly Parkway at 1080 ft, which are the second highest and the lowest of the six. The four that delivered span 1127 to 1167 ft, and include the highest site of the morning, Nittany Avenue. The whole spread is 93 ft over paths of 4 to 6 km.

Distance does not explain it either. Nittany at 6.2 km delivered, Elixer at 6.3 km failed six times, and Alpha at 6.5 km delivered. Westerly failed at 5.3 km while Luna delivered at 4.9 km and Nittany at 6.2 km.

A path blocked by terrain would be blocked both ways. The handheld’s own message from inside Elixer Cafe was copied direct by my station at 09:54:35, no digipeater, street level, 6.3 km through downtown. That one carried 5 W uphill while six transmissions in the other direction arrived nowhere.

The one real height difference

Elixer Cafe and the Fraser Street garage geocode 43 m apart. The cafe’s sidewalk is 3.6 m higher than the garage’s ground level. Five floors up, the garage deck stands roughly 8 m above that sidewalk, and above the rooflines of the block.

What is left

All six failures happened at a single address, so they are six samples of one place rather than six samples of a condition. That limits everything here.

Within that limit, Elixer differs from the sites that worked in two ways: I was on foot rather than in a vehicle, and it was at ground level in a downtown block rather than five floors up or out on the corridor. Mobility is out, because the Fraser garage was parked. Ground elevation is out, because Nittany Avenue is 18 ft higher than Elixer and delivered.

Of the two that remain, height fits the direction of the result and being out of the vehicle does not. A rubber duck inside a car body, in the car’s noise, is a worse receive setup than the same antenna in open air on a sidewalk, so if that were the cause the on-foot site should have done better rather than going 0 for 6. A parking deck five floors up clears the rooflines of a block that a sidewalk sits inside.

The cheap next test is three messages from the Fraser garage ground floor and three from its fifth floor, same morning, parked in the car both times. That holds location, posture, mobility and the antenna’s surroundings fixed and moves only height, and it compares against a condition already known to work.

Which digipeaters repeat my gated traffic

A digipeater that repeats one of my packets usually puts its own callsign in the path and transmits it where I can hear it, so the logs can be counted.

BEACON_VIA = WIDE1-1 is one hop and TX_VIA = WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2 is three, which makes the two classes separable: an echo whose path contains WIDE2 is gated traffic, and an echo with only WIDE1* is a beacon.

W3YA-1 repeated 18 of my beacons and zero of my gated messages, across 28 gated transmissions whose first path element is the same WIDE1-1 the beacons use. Three ordinary explanations do not fit. The same split shows up in an older capture. A day of frames from October 30, on the 15 ft mast, holds 306 echoes of my beacons, 113 of them from W3YA-1, alongside 13 echoes of my gated traffic and none from W3YA-1.

Two explanations the logs rule out

My first guess was the TCPIP in the payload. Two things argue against it.

On the protocol, TCPIP never appears in the digipeater path of a frame on the air. A gated message is wrapped as a third-party packet, so the string sits in the information field after the :}. The AX.25 address fields a digipeater acts on read WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2 and nothing else. Filtering on payload content is a different capability, and a Kantronics KPC-3 does not appear to have it.

On the measurement, W3YA-1 repeated 39 third-party frames in that October day, other gateways’ downlink traffic, all of them carrying TCPIP in the payload:

N3TJJ-13>APMI06,W3YA-1,WIDE1*
KC3ZLD-10>APDW17,NJ3T-4,W3YA-1,WIDE2*
KD3BAA-2>APDW18,NJ3T-4,W3YA-1,WIDE2*

My second guess was a refusal of three-hop paths. In the same archive, W3YA-1 repeated a WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2 path from a mobile tracker:

KG4DQH>APT311,W3YA-1,WIDE1*,WIDE2-2:!4052.49N/07746.52W>234/052

The station asked for three hops and W3YA-1 took the first one, so a hard hop-count ceiling does not look like the rule.

Frame length

W3YA-1 accepts third-party packets, accepts three-hop paths, and accepts my beacons, while refusing my gated traffic, which is the first two combined. The remaining difference I could see is length. My gated messages are the longest frames I transmit: a third-party wrapper, an embedded header and an SMS body, roughly twice a beacon. KG4DQH’s three-hop frame that W3YA-1 did repeat is a bare position report.

This one is also cheap to measure, which the other two were not.

The length test

The test transmits pairs of frames that are identical except for padding. Each cycle sends a 75-byte control, the length of my ordinary beacon, then a test frame at the length under examination. The control says whether the path to the digipeater was working in that cycle, so a missing test echo is not confused with a dead minute. Lengths are visited round-robin rather than as a rising staircase, because a monotonic sweep confounds length with time: if conditions sag over the run, the longest frames are the ones sent last and a false cliff appears.

I stopped the run by hand after 14.7 minutes, 30 cycles, 60 frames. The reason is in the channel courtesy section below.

Thirty cycles is too few to decide any single length, so I pooled them:

  • At 75 bytes, W3YA-1 repeated 19 of 34 frames, 56%.
  • At 83 bytes and above, W3YA-1 repeated 0 of 26.

At the 56% rate the controls establish, 26 consecutive misses has a probability of roughly one in two billion. Over the same 26 long frames, KE4MHE-10 repeated 13 and WO3T-1 repeated 7. So W3YA-1 appears to stop somewhere between 75 and 83 bytes on the air, and the other two carry lengths it will not.

A transmit fault cannot produce this pattern. That was the worry a single-frame smoke test raised earlier, and it does not survive the table. Anything wrong on my side, audio level, deviation, power, distortion, reaches every receiver identically, so it cannot single out one digipeater.

What it would do is cost each receiver the same margin, and a corruption-type fault would cost every receiver the same frames. Neither shows up. KE4MHE-10 repeated 21 of 34 frames at 75 bytes and 13 of 26 at 83 through 131, 62% against 50%, which is nearly flat with length on a sample too small to call the difference real. WO3T-1 went from 15 of 34 to 7 of 26, 44% against 27%, a drop in the range that frame duration alone predicts from collisions. My long frames leave the station intact.

Deviation is also measured rather than assumed: 2.8 kHz by Carson’s rule on 2026-10-03, against a 2.5 to 3.0 kHz target, good to about 50 Hz.

My receiver looks fine too. A W3YA-1 repeat of an 83-byte frame is 90 bytes, because a digipeater inserts its own callsign. W3YA-1’s own beacons run about 86 bytes bare, 93 carrying an unused WIDE2-2, and 100 after a hop, and I decode all three shapes dozens of times in the same window.

It fits the original observation. My beacon is 75 bytes, just under, so W3YA-1 repeats it. My gated traffic runs 91 to 150 bytes, depending on the message text, and W3YA-1 has never repeated any of it.

A configured length limit at W3YA-1 is ruled out. Counting only the October 30 archive’s frames where W3YA-1 was the first hop, so it heard them off the air directly, it repeated 2,269 frames at a median length of 84 bytes, 1,139 of them over 82 and 600 over 90.

The cliff is steeper than a bit-error model predicts. Fitting a per-bit error rate to the 56% control rate at 75 bytes predicts 53% at 83 bytes, and 0 of 26 is about as improbable against that as against the control rate itself. Independent bit errors are the wrong model for a decoder that recovers its bit clock in hardware and corrects nothing, which is what the next section establishes W3YA-1 to be.

What that digipeater can be configured to do

W3YA-1 and K3DNA both transmit with the tocall APN391. The APRS tocall registry maps the whole APN3xx range to Kantronics KPC-3 ROM versions, and aprs.fi lists W3YA-1’s device as a Kantronics KPC-3. The identical tocall means the same model running the same ROM.

The KPC-3 Plus user guide gives six parameters that bear on repeating a UI frame: DIGIPEAT, MYCALL, MYALIAS, UIDIGI, UIFLOOD and UITRACE, with UIDWAIT controlling only whether a delay is inserted before transmission. Every one matches on a call sign or an alias in the AX.25 address field. UIDIGI “may be used to set up to four additional aliases/call signs for ‘special’ digipeating service,” and the match is on the address.

PACLEN is the only length parameter in the TNC. It takes 0 to 255 and governs how data arriving from the serial port is cut into packets, not what the TNC repeats. BUDLIST and SUPLIST choose which received packets are reported to the terminal and do not touch digipeating.

So nothing in the TNC reads the information field, which means it cannot know a frame’s APRS data type, and nothing accepts or rejects a frame by length. A KPC-3 cannot be configured to repeat short frames and drop long ones, whatever their type.

K3DNA is the control, and it is as close to matched as this gets. Same tocall, so the same model and ROM, and it repeated 12 of the same 50 gated frames at 91 bytes that W3YA-1 took none of. Identical hardware, opposite result, same day, same transmitter.

What is left

Three explanations survive, and all of them sit at the far end:

  • The receive audio level into that TNC. A KPC-3 recovers its bit clock with a hardware modem and has no error correction. Fed too much or too little audio from its receiver, its error rate per bit rises while short frames still get through, and a 91-byte frame has 21% more bits to lose than a 75-byte one. Nobody outside the site can read that setting.
  • Site noise and co-channel traffic. A 2065 ft ridge hears a horizon a valley station does not, so its receiver meets interference that leaves no trace in my log. That cost rises with how long a frame holds the channel.
  • A marginal signal from me at that receiver in particular. W3YA-1 repeats 29% of my 75-byte beacons in the October log and 32% in November. Over 5.4 miles to a mountaintop that is a poor rate in absolute terms, whatever it looks like next to the other digipeaters, and a decoder without error correction falls off steeply from a base that low.

None of the three is separable from this end, and that is the finding rather than a gap in it. Every explanation that my own station could fix has been eliminated: a transmit fault is common to all receivers and the other two show no length penalty worth the name, deviation measures in band, and height changed nothing across two captures at two antenna heights. What is left needs either more power, which is a margin question and not a fault, or the operator at the other end.

At the 32% rate the 75-byte beacons establish, 0 of the 128 frames over 82 bytes has a probability near 1e-22. The sweep contributes 105 of those 128, so the figure is not independent of it. Dropping every frame at a length the sweep used, and adding October’s 50 gated frames at 91 bytes from a week before the sweep ran, leaves 0 of 73 ordinary transmissions over 82 bytes against a pooled beacon rate of 31%, at 2e-12.

The 79-byte row is the one I would like more of. Ten frames with no repeats is a probability of 0.02 at the base rate, which puts the cliff below 83 bytes rather than at it. Ten frames do not settle a rate, so that stays a hint.

Same length, same digipeater, same day. On October 30, at exactly 91 bytes on the air, W3YA-1 repeated 96 frames from K3DNA and one from K3ARL-6, and none of the 50 that KD3CCO-10 sent. The October beacon rate was 113 of 394, 29%, so 0 of 50 has a probability near 5e-8. Nothing in the frames distinguishes the two stations. The signal arriving at the digipeater does.

The sweep was not needed. It put 60 frames on a shared channel to measure what two archived days already held, because my gated traffic varies in length on its own. The station’s own log was the cheaper instrument and I reached for it second.

W3YA-1 does not carry my messaging at the power and antenna of that run. The third-party wrapper alone is about 41 bytes, so even a one-character gated message runs about 75 bytes on a one-hop path and 82 on two. Shortening TX_VIA from three hops to two buys 7 bytes.

The handheld’s uplink rides a 49 W mountaintop digipeater at 640 ft. Its downlink does not, because that digipeater does not repeat my frames at message length. What is left for the downlink is my own signal, WO3T-1 and KE4MHE-10, and the handheld heard none of the three from a downtown sidewalk.

Still untested: power

There is one more experiment, and my station already runs the probe for it. igate-selftest.timer fires every four hours and puts one gated frame on the air each time, at the real length. Setting the radio from its low power step to high and waiting a day gives about six probes a day at the length that matters, with no added traffic. If W3YA-1,WIDE1*,WIDE2-2 starts appearing in my log, power moves the cliff. If it stays absent while WO3T-1 and KE4MHE-10 keep showing up, the margin at W3YA-1 is short of what power alone recovers.

Raising power costs nothing on the channel. A 120-byte frame occupies the same slot at 5 W as at 75 W.

The archive check is done, and raising power is the one lever left at this end. It does not test a fault, since there is no evidence of one. It tests whether W3YA-1’s margin on my signal is thin enough that 10 dB moves the cliff. Both captures put every one of my frames over 82 bytes at zero repeats, 178 frames across the two, while W3YA-1 carried other stations past 90 bytes in both. The 15 ft mount and the mast give the same answer, so height is not the knob either.

My own radio notes carry an earlier attempt at this, concluding that “power is not the knob; antenna height is.” That was measured on an indoor antenna, with W3YA-1 repeating 2 of 8 beacons where it now repeats most of them, and the metric was a success rate at one frame length, which cannot see a cliff move. I am treating it as stale rather than settled.

What this does not establish

  • Which copy the handheld decoded. The FT5D knows and does not report it. Every delivered message is equally consistent with my direct signal or a repeat.
  • That the handheld can hear W3YA-1. Digipeating is one-way evidence. W3YA-1 hears the handheld; the reverse is untested, and the proposed fix rests on it.
  • Any rate for anything seen once. The Cortland path and W3VO-1’s two repeats are existence proofs.
  • Which digipeaters heard me and declined. One suppressing a duplicate looks like one that never heard the packet.
  • What makes the cliff sharp. A bit-error-rate rolloff predicts a gradual decline and the sweep shows none. Sync-threshold failure and a length-dependent fault in my own transmitter both fit, and these logs do not choose between them.
  • Why the cafe failed. The handheld’s own message from inside it was heard direct, so its transmitter reached me from there. Coverage, the handheld’s receiver and the downtown noise floor are not separable in these logs.
  • Where the radio was when a message arrived. Each message was sent up to three times over several minutes while the car was moving. The site labels name where a message was sent from, not where it was received.
  • How much a better antenna would help. The rubber duck was constant, so it cannot explain the difference between groups, but it could be holding the whole downlink down. A mag mount and a rerun would say.

The blind spot

Message {17} was acknowledged by the gateway and appears in none of my logs. Another gateway heard the handheld and carried it, and reading my own logs would never show that. The same hole runs the other way: a digipeater that repeats my traffic from outside my receive range leaves no trace.

The Cortland chain got through that hole by accident. I learned that KE4MHE-10, N3TJJ-7 and KC2ANT-1 had repeated one of my messages because the packet came back from APRS-IS, gated in New York, with the whole path attached. I never heard that echo on the air.

It arrived with two bit errors: TCPYP for TCPIP, and KD3CCO-1p* for KD3CCO-10*. Loop detection drops a third-party packet whose inner path carries the station’s own callsign, and the corruption put that callsign past recognition, so the packet was gated straight back to RF inside a second layer of encapsulation. The whitelist then passed it, correctly: g/ matches a message’s addressee, and the message inside was addressed to KD3CCO-7, which is on the whitelist and has to be for the downlink to exist at all. An earlier version of this paragraph said the term matched the outer source. It does not, and narrowing the whitelist to explicit SSIDs would not have stopped this. The control that failed was the loop check.

It happened once and went no further. Each pass adds a layer, so a sustained loop would show as growing brace nesting in the transmit lines, which is now something I check. Two guards would close it properly. The outer source of the returning packet was KD3CCO-10, uncorrupted, and this station never needs to retransmit a frame it sourced itself, so FILTER IG 0 ! b/KD3CCO-10 rejects the loop on a field the bit errors did not touch. Nesting depth is the other: a gated message carries one layer of encapsulation, so refusing anything with two is a check no corrupted callsign can defeat.

On channel courtesy

144.390 is one shared FM channel for the whole of North America, and nobody allocates slots on it. My goal is simple enough to state: stay below the median of whatever the local norm is. I wanted to know what other digipeaters and iGates are around me and whether I could keep my own traffic under theirs.

My own receiver can answer that. It has been logging every frame it can decode for months. From the 8.2-day October archive:

  • 29,800 frames decoded, of which only 4,785 were originals. 84% of what I hear is digipeater repeats of other people’s packets.
  • 3,634 frames a day, about 151 an hour, arriving at my antenna.
  • 22 stations heard directly.

Most of those 22 are mobiles and handhelds passing through, sitting at a median of 0.9 packets a day, which makes the all-stations median of 1.3 useless as a yardstick. The stations I actually want to compare against are the permanent ones. I picked them out by asking which callsigns show up as a used digipeater somewhere in the window, which is a rough proxy and the best one the log offers:

Where I sit

Counting my own transmissions plus the repeats my paths provoke, measured at 0.78 repeats per one-hop beacon and 0.26 per three-hop gated frame in the same window:

I moved the beacon to two hours on the day of these measurements. At 30 minutes I was transmitting nearly twice as often as the median resident digipeater, which is hard to justify for a fixed site whose position has never changed. At two hours I sit below that median, fourth quietest of the nine resident stations counting myself.

I considered four hours and left it alone. The resident distribution has a gap between 1.2 and 19.5 packets a day, so 18 and 12 land on the same percentile, and the trade is 0.3 points of channel occupancy against half the remaining sample in every measurement I make of my own station.

Eight stations is a small population and the digipeating proxy misses any receive-only gateway. The ordering seems clear enough regardless: I went from above the median to below it.

What the length test cost

The sweep put 2 frames on the air every 30 seconds. That is 240 originals an hour, and at the measured one-hop repeat rate it provokes about 187 more. Against a local channel carrying roughly 151 frames an hour, I would have been around three quarters of everything on the air for the duration of the run.

That is why I cut it off at 14.7 minutes instead of the hour I had budgeted. The pooled numbers were already decisive, and I had not thought hard enough about what 4 frames a minute meant before starting.

The remaining power question needs no new frames at all, since a timer already puts six gated probes a day on the air. Running the baseline measurement first would have told me all of this before I wrote the test.

What the morning was worth

An uplink that never failed and a downlink that reached the radio four times in ten, same radio, same morning, roughly the same few square miles. The asymmetry was already in my notes as a prediction. What was missing was the reason, and the logs narrow it: the one digipeater the handheld reliably reaches is the one that will not repeat what my gateway sends, apparently because the frames are too long. The downtown block of six failures is the part still open. The South Atherton corridor already works.

The result I did not expect is which setup worked. The messages that arrived went to a rubber duck shielded inside a car. The ones that failed went to the same radio held in open air.

One other thing came out of this that has nothing to do with radio. My logs gave a confident answer about which messages were delivered and it was wrong by a factor of two, because it was answering a different question. Reading the radio took a minute and changed the result.
