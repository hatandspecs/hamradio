The Digipeater That Cannot Relay My Messages

Published on: November 14, 2026  · 

W3YA-1 relays other stations’ frames up to 102 bytes on the air. It relays none of mine above 82 bytes as sent.

W3YA-1 relaying copies min median max emitted
For other stations 19,733 42 80 102
For KD3CCO-10 556 72 82 89

Those two rows are the article. W3YA-1 does have an unusually low ceiling, at 102 bytes: it emitted 220 copies above 97 and 3 at 102. But my traffic never reaches it. The longest gated message this station sends, 150 bytes, would be relayed at 157; the shortest, 91 bytes, at 98, which is under 102 and still never happens. W3YA-1 decoded a 91-byte frame from K3ARL-6 and 2,048 frames from NJ3T-4 above 82 bytes as sent. My own frames stop dead at 82, thirteen bytes below its own ceiling.

That ceiling is still worth reporting, because W3YA-1 is an outlier among its neighbors. Every digipeater this receiver hears, measured the same way:

The cap is on the whole frame, and the hop count does not move it

Grouping W3YA-1’s emissions by its own hop position separates three possibilities, because at hop k the frame already carries k−1 inserted callsigns and so is longer for the same information field.

That is the signature of a limit on the entire frame, and it is a second, independent refutation of PACLEN, which would hold the field constant and let the frame grow.

My blocked frames sit inside that envelope

The shortest message this station can gate is 91 bytes on WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2: two path entries and a 57-byte information field. W3YA-1 taking it at hop 1 would emit 98 bytes with three path entries and the same 57-byte field.

At three path entries, W3YA-1 has emitted 68 copies at exactly 98 bytes, 200 copies at 98 bytes or longer, and 200 copies carrying a field of 57 bytes or more, to a maximum of 102 bytes and a 61-byte field. Every dimension of the frame it will not relay for me is one it demonstrably emits, repeatedly, at the same path length.

So no length rule of any kind accounts for this station’s zero. The cap is real and anomalous; my traffic never reaches it.

KD3CCO-10 as sent W3YA-1 relayed Sent Rate
82 bytes and below 556 2,250 25%
above 82 bytes 0 304 0%

So the cause is none of the obvious three. Not its length ceiling, because that sits at 102 and my frames would be relayed at 98. Not a configured limit matching my frames either, because W3YA-1 carries other stations 9 bytes past where it stops carrying me. Not a signal margin, because a graded path cannot produce a step: the bit error rate that leaves 25% of my 82-byte frames surviving leaves about 22% at 91 bytes, not 0 of 164. The effect is specific to the path from this station into that receiver, and the mechanism is unresolved.

The downlink is not blocked. It is blocked at W3YA-1. WO3T-1 and KE4MHE-10 relay my full-length gated messages, to 150 bytes, and four more digipeaters relay them at 91. One of the four is K3DNA, which announces the same APN391 Kantronics KPC-3+ firmware as W3YA-1 and sits 26 km away against W3YA-1’s 10. On 2026-10-30 this station sent 50 gated frames at 91 bytes: K3DNA repeated 12 of them and W3YA-1 repeated none. Across the whole twelve-log archive K3DNA has taken 20 of my frames over 82 bytes at first hop, against W3YA-1’s zero. The two figures are different populations, one day and twelve logs, and both appear below.

The 7-byte insertion is what converts that into a limit on senders. W3YA-1 inserts its callsign ahead of the alias it consumed, which adds an address field. My beacon goes out as KD3CCO-10>APDW17,WIDE1-1 and comes back as KD3CCO-10>APDW17,W3YA-1,WIDE1*. So a frame of 90 bytes as sent becomes 97 as relayed and 91 becomes 98, which W3YA-1 has done 73 times for other stations. The insertion sets the arithmetic, not a limit.

The cliff, measured on purpose

This is the part I cannot close, and with the ceiling gone it is the only effect left to explain.

A length sweep of 141 frames, everything held constant except padding, on a path naming W3YA-1 by callsign, addressed to an SSID nothing answers:

Sent on-air Relayed as Relayed

Every zero row is unexplained, because W3YA-1 emits all of those lengths for other people. The 86-byte row would be relayed as 93 bytes, and W3YA-1 emitted 1,373 copies at exactly 93 bytes for NJ3T-4 alone. The 97-byte row would be relayed as 104, and W3YA-1 emits to 102. Across 92 to 100 bytes emitted it relayed for NJ3T-4, WV8MT-4, WD4ITN-4, KA2NDW-1, W3NEP-2 and others, and for my station not once.

Nor is the information field the variable, which rules out PACLEN directly. Holding the field at 55 bytes and changing only the digipeater count, which costs 7 bytes of header and nothing in the field, 82 on-air bytes was relayed 6 of 15 and 89 on-air bytes 0 of 15. The whole frame is the variable, not the field PACLEN governs.

So the effect is station-selective, and a length cap cannot be station-selective. A graded path cannot produce a step either. What is left is something specific to this station’s signal arriving at that receiver that acts like a threshold rather than like margin, and nothing in the record identifies it.

Not the path settings either

A digipeater can be configured to answer some path requests and ignore others, and this station’s beacons and its gated messages ask for different things: BEACON_VIA = WIDE1-1 against TX_VIA = WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2. A UIFLOOD or UITRACE limit that accepted one hop and refused three would reproduce the whole beacon-against-message split with no length effect at all.

W3YA-1 answers every alias form this station uses. Counted over its 6,730 first-hop relays for other stations, by the alias it consumed:

WIDE1 2,581
WIDE2, exhausted 553
WIDE2-2, decremented to WIDE2-1 3,666
its own callsign, named explicitly 3,030

So there is no max-hop limit of one, and no refusal of WIDE2-2. The exact two-alias form this station transmits is rare rather than rejected: only three frames from other stations in the whole archive carry WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2, and W3YA-1 relayed all three.

The sweep settles it, because it gave the digipeater no path to object to. Its frames named W3YA-1 as a single path entry, with no alias and no flood counter, leaving nothing for a configuration to match on but the callsign itself:

And the second hop works when the frame is short enough. Once W3YA-1 consumes the WIDE1-1, the trailing WIDE2-2 is picked up normally: W3YA-1 to KE4MHE-10 twenty-two times and W3YA-1 to WO3T-1 twenty times for this station’s own traffic.

Not my transmitted signal, except in one specific way

A defect in what this station transmits, worsening as a frame runs on, was the best-fitting idea left after power went. Most of it is wrong, and the test is a distinction the earlier tables did not draw: a first-hop relay means that digipeater demodulated my own signal, while a later-hop relay only means it copied another digipeater’s clean retransmission and says nothing about me.

First-hop relays of this station’s frames over 82 bytes as sent:

Digipeater Distance Frames over 82 B Longest as sent
KE4MHE-10 56 km 119 131
WO3T-1 42 km 93 150
K3DNA 26 km 20 91
W3VO-1 60 km 9 91
N3SNN-3 74 km 3 91
N3DXC-2 157 km 1 91
W3YA-1 10 km 0 —

Six receivers demodulated my long frames straight off the air, 245 times between them, and the nearest one has never managed it. A defect that accumulates over a frame would cost the distant, weaker-signal receivers first; these are the receivers it does not trouble. K3DNA settles the hardware version of the idea on its own: same APN391 KPC-3+ firmware as W3YA-1, 2.6 times the distance, 20 of my long frames decoded directly.

That excludes the signal-to-noise members of the family, and only those. A defect whose cost depends on received level is refuted by the table: these receivers have less margin than W3YA-1 and more success. Supply sag, clipping and anything thermal or arc-like go with it, since all of them scale with drive and none of them survived 5 W either.

It does not exclude a level-independent defect, and FM is where that distinction matters. Past the limiter, recovered audio amplitude is set by deviation rather than by received power, so a receiver at 157 km with adequate signal recovers the same waveform as one at 10 km. A defect that lives in the audio waveform is therefore invisible to distance, and the first-hop table says nothing about it. Carrier frequency error and mark/space deviation asymmetry are both of that kind, and the occupied-bandwidth check that measured 2.8 kHz in October sees neither: it measures total deviation, not its symmetry or its center.

Nor a race it keeps losing

One mechanism survived that far: for a WIDE1-1 frame only one digipeater gets the hop, so if a competitor transmits first, W3YA-1 is left looking at an already-repeated frame with nothing to act on. Who takes the first hop on this station’s frames:

First hop ≤82 B (n=1,407) >82 B (n=129)
WO3T-1 48% 23%
nobody 21% 69%
W3YA-1 20% 0%
KE4MHE-10 10% 8%

It is not a race, because the race has no single winner. In 228 of my frames at 82 bytes or under, W3YA-1 and a competitor both repeated the same frame, so W3YA-1 plainly does not stand down because somebody else went first. And in 40 cases a competitor repeated one of my frames over 82 bytes while W3YA-1 declined, which puts that frame on the air, demodulated, and available to it.

Nor the bit pattern inside the frame

If the cliff is a threshold crossed at an elapsed time, the obvious driver is the bit pattern: a standing offset or a strongly periodic stream walks a slicer’s running mark/space midpoint until the eye closes. The padding in every sweep up to this point was a repeated ., so every earlier zero was recorded with one pattern.

Three patterns at the same 90 bytes, interleaved within every cycle so that pattern never co-varies with time:

Padding at 90 B Sent W3YA-1 relayed
. repeated, 0x2E 12 0
U repeated, 0x55, equal time in mark and space 12 0
pseudo-random printable, balanced and aperiodic 12 0
all three 36 0
control, 74 B 12 4

The control held at a third, so the digipeater was answering throughout. A one-tailed Fisher exact test of 0 of 36 against that 4-of-12 control gives p = 0.0025, about one in 400.

So the frame’s contents are not the variable, only its length. A standing offset or a spectral line would have been relieved by one of the two balanced patterns, and neither moved a single frame. What survives of the audio-waveform idea is the part no bit pattern can touch: carrier frequency error, mark-to-space deviation asymmetry, and sound-card clock drift, which accumulates with elapsed time whatever the bits are doing.

Against all three of those stands K3DNA, which decodes this station at 91 bytes on the same APN391 firmware from 2.6 times the distance. Any defect in the transmitted signal meeting an APN391 demodulator should trouble it too. That points past the firmware at the installation: the audio level set into that particular TNC, the receiver and audio path behind it, or the RF environment of that one mountaintop.

Method

The station. A Yaesu FT-2900R at its Low3 setting, 30 W, through about 50 feet of RG-8X into an N9TAX monoband 2 m Slim Jim on a mast measured at 27 feet, feeding a Raspberry Pi running Dire Wolf. W3YA-1 is 10.3 km away, 6.4 miles, on Pine Grove Mountain, 49 W into an 8 dBi omni at 640 feet above average terrain by its own PHG beacon. It is a Kantronics KPC-3.

Every length here is an on-air length: 7 bytes per address, control, PID, information field, opening flag, FCS, closing flag. That is the arithmetic this project’s existing tooling uses, and mixing it with any other definition is how I spent an afternoon producing numbers two bytes off from every table I already had.

The byte counting is exact, and two things make that non-trivial. Dire Wolf prints unprintable bytes as <0xNN>, six logged characters standing for one on-air byte, and it does that for spaces too: <0x20> appears 10,241 times in this archive, alongside 38,826 carriage returns and 10,849 Mic-E <0x1c> bytes. Each decodes to one byte. The logs must also be read as Latin-1 rather than UTF-8, because 199 frames carry byte sequences that are valid multi-byte UTF-8, and decoding those as text counts two bytes as one character. Both are checked against hand-counted frames in digi_relay_audit.py. The 11 lines Dire Wolf marks (Not AX.25) are excluded; they are noise decodes with no address field.

The archive did the heavy lifting and cost no airtime. The full Pi archive, 166,803 frames across twelve Dire Wolf logs from 2026-10-27 to 2026-11-15, is where the per-station comparison comes from. One day of it is not enough: a single 29,800-frame log puts the emission ceiling at 97 rather than 102 and the cliff at 0 of 50 rather than 0 of 304. The sweep contributed the controls the archive cannot supply, which are the ones below.

The sweep’s controls. Each cycle visited every length in a fresh random order, because a rising sweep confounds length with time: if conditions sag, the longest frames go last and a false cliff appears. Each cycle also carried a frame at the shortest length, since a digipeater that is relaying nothing at all produces the same result as one refusing long frames. A run whose control cell came back empty gets discarded, and one did.

The measurement is my own receiver copying my own transmission with W3YA-1* marked used. Only frames confirmed transmitted in the log are counted, because IGTXLIMIT drops gated frames over 6 per minute rather than queueing them, and a dropped frame would otherwise read as a digipeater that declined.

Four explanations the sweep ruled out

The shortest cell relayed 16 of 35 frames that named W3YA-1 by callsign, carried a third-party wrap, and were message frames. So W3YA-1 answers its own callsign rather than only WIDE aliases, does not filter third-party traffic, and does not treat messages differently from beacons.

The fourth is the information field, which is not what is counted. Over the sweep alone, holding the field at 55 bytes and changing only the digipeater count, 82 bytes was relayed 4 of 9 and 89 bytes 0 of 10; the same contrast over the whole archive is 6 of 15 against 0 of 15. Either way the header is what moved and the field did not, so a PACLEN-style limit on the data portion is out.

None of the four is the mechanism, and neither are the path settings.

Power is not the knob, measured across 12 dB

Three power levels, same station, same digipeater, and the cliff does not move.

Power Control, below the cliff 86 B 90 B
5 W (Low1) 5 of 15 at 74 B 0 of 15 0 of 15
30 W (Low3) 10 of 22 at 74 B 0 of 12 0 of 15
75 W (High) 3 of 12 — 0 of 18

The 5 W run is the one that matters, because it is the only test in the downward direction and the only one that can refute receiver overload. At 10 km through a Slim Jim at 27 ft, this station lands roughly −40 dBm at W3YA-1, which is inside the range where a front end can compress, and compression would look like a degraded path without being one. Cutting 7.8 dB off it changed nothing: the 74-byte control still came back a third of the time, and the long frames still never came back at all. Overload is out, and so is any mechanism that eases with less drive, including supply sag pulling the transmitter during a long frame.

Raising the radio from Low3 to High is 4.0 dB in the other direction. Above the threshold, 0 of 24 at 30 W and 0 of 18 at 75 W. Below it, 15 of 29 against 3 of 12, one-tailed Fisher p = 0.11, which is no change either.

Pooling the two runs on the single-entry W3YA-1 path, where the digipeater answers 41 percent of control frames, gives 0 of 57 above the cliff. Against the 15-of-37 control that sets that baseline, a one-tailed Fisher exact test gives p = 1.0e-7, about one in ten million.

A 5 W handheld makes the same point from the other side. In the 69 to 82 byte range where both radios transmitted, W3YA-1 relayed 6 of 14 of the handheld’s frames and 42 of 95 of this station’s: 43 percent against 44, for a sixth of the power through a rubber duck inside a car. The handheld never sent anything longer than 80 bytes, so it says nothing about the cliff itself, but a link on which 7.8 dB and a far better antenna buy nothing is not a link limited by signal strength.

A larger step was already on record. Moving this gateway from a handheld to the FT-2900R was 8 dB and produced no measurable improvement either; the limit then turned out to be the antenna, and replacing a mobile whip indoors with the Slim Jim is what moved the numbers.

The power setting is an assumption rather than a measurement. There is no CAT port, the level was set by hand, and nothing in the log records transmit power. The three levels are the radio’s own Low1, Low3 and High, nominally 5, 30 and 75 W.

The link budget, and why it is not an argument

Free-space path loss over 10.3 km at 144.39 MHz is 95.9 dB. With the Slim Jim at a nominal 4.6 dBi, 2 dB for the feedline, and W3YA-1’s self-reported 8 dBi, the signal arriving there is about -40 dBm against roughly -110 dBm for reliable 1200 baud packet. That is near 70 dB of margin, and 30 dB for terrain and clutter still leaves 39.

I first read that as proof signal strength is not the variable. It is not proof of anything of the kind. A budget predicting 70 dB of margin against a 29% repeat rate is a budget that disagrees with the measurement, and the measurement wins. What it establishes is that something unmodelled is costing a great deal.

What this does not establish

What sets W3YA-1’s 102-byte ceiling, and whether it is related. It is far below its neighbors and below K3DNA on identical firmware, so something at that site produces it. It cannot be the direct cause of my problem, because my frames would be relayed at 98, under it. Whether the same cause also produces an 89-byte cutoff for one station is not something the record answers. The KPC-3’s documented digipeat parameters are DIGIPEAT, MYCALL, MYALIAS, UIDIGI, UIFLOOD, UITRACE and UIDWAIT, all matching on a callsign or alias, and PACLEN is scoped by its manual to data arriving from the serial port. None of them is a relayed-frame length cap, and the sweep rules PACLEN out by measurement anyway, since the whole frame rather than the field is the variable. So if either limit is configuration, it is not a documented setting.

What the station-selective cliff is. Not margin, and that deserves stating plainly because I listed it as a candidate for most of a day. AX.25 carries no error correction, so frame survival is (1 - BER) raised to the bit count, and the bit error rate that yields 44% at 82 bytes yields about 40% at 86, not zero. No bit error rate fits both cells. A graded path cannot produce a step, so the 29% repeat rate on short frames and the hard zero on longer ones are two different phenomena and only the first is margin.

A step implies a deterministic threshold. There were two families of candidate: a cap in that receiver, below its own 102-byte one and somehow specific to this station, or something in this station’s transmitted signal that worsens with frame duration until it crosses that receiver’s decode threshold.

The second family is closed except for its level-independent members. Its power-dependent members went first: any defect that eases with less drive, supply sag pulling the transmit frequency being the obvious one, should have improved at 5 W, and nothing did. The first-hop table took the rest of the level-dependent ones. Six receivers from 26 to 157 km demodulated these frames at up to 150 bytes, and a defect whose cost rises as signal falls would have hurt them before it hurt the nearest receiver of the eight.

What is left is a rule in that one receiver that singles out this station. The level-independent half of the second family is still standing, but it no longer has a mechanism that produces a step.

The mechanism it had was slicer creep. A data slicer holds a running estimate of the midpoint between mark and space; feed it a waveform with a standing offset or a strongly periodic bit pattern and that estimate walks, closing the eye until a bit mis-slices and the frame fails its check. That is a threshold crossed at an elapsed time, and the cliff is a time: 82 on-air bytes is 0.55 seconds at 1200 baud, and it sits at the same on-air length on all four path forms rather than at a fixed information length.

The padding sweep tested it and it did not survive. Three patterns at the same 90 bytes, two of them DC-balanced and one of those aperiodic, 0 of 36 relayed while the 74-byte control ran at a third. A creeping slicer would have been relieved by a balanced pattern. None of the three moved a frame.

What remains of the audio-waveform idea is carrier frequency error and mark-to-space deviation asymmetry, which no bit pattern can touch. Neither is length-dependent, so neither produces a step on its own: a static defect raises the per-bit error rate uniformly and runs into the same arithmetic that excluded margin, where 44% at 82 bytes gives about 40% at 86. Sound-card clock drift is the one member of the family that does accumulate with elapsed time, and the magnitude rules it out below.

So the two measurements still worth taking are this station’s carrier frequency error and its mark-to-space deviation symmetry, because both are invisible to the occupied-bandwidth check and neither costs any airtime. They would bound a defect rather than explain the step.

One difference in the chain is worth recording, and it is why the second family cannot be closed outright. The iGate modulates from Dire Wolf through a Digirig into the radio’s audio input, where the handheld uses the radio’s own modem, and frequency offset, deviation asymmetry, clipping and sound-card bit timing are all invisible to the occupied-bandwidth check that measured 2.8 kHz deviation in October. The first-hop table disposes of the level-dependent ones: clipping and anything that scales with drive would cost the six receivers between 26 and 157 km before the nearest one, and those six demodulate this station at up to 150 bytes. Sound-card bit timing goes on magnitude, since accumulated timing error reaching half a bit period over a 573 ms frame needs roughly 700 ppm, far more than a sound card should drift, and an AFSK demodulator resynchronizes on every transition rather than integrating error across a frame. Frequency offset and deviation asymmetry are the two nothing here rules out, and every frame over 82 bytes in this record was modulated by this one chain: the handheld has never sent one longer than 80 bytes.

A confound worth naming before anyone compares these populations. This station’s beacons go out on BEACON_VIA = WIDE1-1 and its gated messages on TX_VIA = WIDE1-1,WIDE2-2. Those offer different aliases, so different digipeaters answer them, which is why K3DNA repeats 6% of the long frames and 0.8% of the short ones, and why W3YA-1 serves the beacon path 59% of the time and the message path 20%. Any beacon-against-message comparison carries that contamination. The sweep held the path fixed and named W3YA-1 directly, which is why it is the better evidence of the two, and why the path settings are ruled out rather than merely set aside.

Three sweeps, three power levels across 12 dB, three padding patterns, one antenna. The per-station comparison rests on the full archive, 166,803 frames across twelve logs. The cliff is 0 of 304 above 82 bytes against 556 of 2,250 at or below, and the sweep is the only part of it with the path held fixed by design.

Four times I reached the wrong conclusion

Worth recording, because each was a different failure.

I asserted a configuration cause, then asserted its opposite, before measuring either. First I predicted PACLEN in the low 80s and drafted an email asking the digipeater’s manager to read it. Then, finding the project’s own archive analysis, I swung to “configuration is dead” and said the email should not be sent. Both were positions taken from notes rather than from data, and the measurements since have retired both: PACLEN is ruled out by the 55-byte field test, and so is every other configured length limit, because W3YA-1 carries other stations past where it stops carrying me.

I ran a 141-frame sweep that the project’s notes said not to run, at a cost of three hours of occupancy on a shared channel. It did produce the four controls above, which the archive cannot. It produced nothing about the cause.

I used a byte scale two short of every existing table, by omitting the flags and FCS, so none of my figures could be compared with measurements already banked until they were converted.

I put the ceiling at 97 bytes from one day of a twelve-log archive, and built the conclusion on it. The full archive carries 220 relayed copies past 97, to 102. The ceiling is real, but 97 was close enough to my own 91-byte messages to make a one-byte argument look decisive, and 102 is not: the messages fit under it with room to spare. A limit measured to within 5 bytes cannot carry an argument that turns on 1, and the caveat naming one log as one log should have outranked the finding it qualified.

Tooling faults found along the way

The station’s self-test had reported FAIL at step 3: the gateway had the message and did not transmit it every four hours for six days, while the log showed the transmission one second after each report. Adding -T to the Dire Wolf invocation put the timestamp inside the existing bracket, so [0L] became [0L 2026-11-14T04:31:52-0500], and twelve log-parsing sites anchored on the old shape stopped matching: the self-test’s transmit and echo checks, the repeater attribution, the monitor’s entire event display, the reach floor, and the spatial analysis. The unit test fixtures still generate the pre--T format, which is why the suite passed throughout.

Three more surfaced once those fired again. The acknowledgement check matched ack36 against a genuine ack3686 and reported a delivery that never happened. The echo attribution named routing aliases rather than stations. And the echo check counted other gateways’ copies of the same message as proof our own frame had been radiated, which it is not: N3DXC-2 and N3TJJ-7 gate the identical message independently, arriving 34 and 40 seconds later.

The worst was mine. My replacement for the attribution printed its fallback on empty input, so the step passed on every run regardless. Only running it caught that, and bash -n never would have.

And the analysis tool failed silently on compressed logs. Handed a .log.gz path directly it read the gzip stream as text, matched nothing, and printed a confident “none”. That is how I nearly answered this whole question backwards. It sniffs the gzip magic now.

Fixing the test fixtures immediately found a thirteenth broken site. Every fixture in the suite used the pre--T bracket shape, which is why six days of broken parsing passed its own tests. Mixing both shapes in made three assertions fail at once, and the cause was the archive script’s four transmit counters, which had been reading zero the whole time. Every daily summary written in that window understates them.

What is next

Ask the digipeater’s manager two separate questions. The first is general: W3YA-1 emits to 102 bytes where its seven neighbors reach 121 to 251, six of them past 200, K3DNA among them on the same APN391 firmware. The second is specific to this station, and it is not PACLEN, which the field test rules out. The measurement to hand over is narrow, and the hop table above is the part to lead with: W3YA-1 emits nothing above 102 bytes at any hop position on 20,000 copies, where its neighbors reach 143 to 228 and K3DNA reaches 224 on the same APN391. Then the station-specific measurement: 0 of 304 frames from KD3CCO-10 above 82 on-air bytes relayed, against 556 of 2,250 at or below, over twelve logs and 166,803 frames, while in the same archive W3YA-1 relayed 2,048 frames for NJ3T-4 above 82 bytes as sent, to 90, and one for K3ARL-6 at 91. That is a specific, falsifiable observation about one station’s signal into his receiver rather than a guess at a setting.

Send a long frame from a different modulator. Every frame over 82 bytes here came from one Dire Wolf and one Digirig, so the measurement cannot separate that chain from the digipeater. The handheld’s own modem, or a frame of the same length from another station’s equipment on the same path, splits the two directly. It is cheaper than measuring the deviation symmetry and it decides more: if a long frame from a second modulator gets relayed, the defect is in this chain, and if it does not, the defect is at W3YA-1.

Compare against K3DNA deliberately. It announces the same APN391 KPC-3+ firmware, sits 26 km out against W3YA-1’s 10, and took 12 of the 50 gated 91-byte frames of one day and 20 over 82 bytes across the archive, where W3YA-1 took none either way. Two identical TNCs disagreeing about the same transmitter is the sharpest handle on this left.

Measure channel occupancy rather than signal. Half of my short frames are not relayed on a link with tens of decibels of margin, which points at a busy receiver rather than a deaf one. Dire Wolf already logs every frame it decodes, so that costs no transmissions.

A Yagi is not worth building for this. It adds 6 to 10 dB to a path that already has far more margin than it needs, and neither a busy receiver on a mountain nor a threshold that ignores a 4 dB power change responds to gain.
